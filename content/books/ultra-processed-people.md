## In short

Chris van Tulleken, an infectious diseases doctor at University College London and a familiar face on BBC children's television, spent a month eating the way many British people do, with 80 per cent of his calories from ultra-processed food. He used the experiment as the thread for a book arguing that such food, not sugar, laziness or weak willpower, is the main driver of the rise in obesity and diet-related disease. The second half turns from the body to the money: who makes this food, how it is sold, and why governments have been slow to act.

## Summary

The book starts with a question that runs through everything that follows: what counts as ultra-processed food, or UPF? He takes the answer from the Brazilian nutrition scientist Carlos Monteiro, whose NOVA system sorts food into four groups by how much it has been processed. The fourth group is UPF. Van Tulleken offers a rough rule of thumb: if it comes wrapped in plastic and contains at least one ingredient you would not find in a home kitchen, it is probably UPF. Think modified starch, maltodextrin, emulsifiers, xanthan gum or "flavourings."

Early on he explains why these ingredients exist. They are cheap, they let a product sit on a shelf for months, and they let manufacturers imitate the texture of cream or butter with far less of the real thing. As an extreme example he tells the story of "coal butter," a fully synthetic fat made from coal in 1930s Germany.

Then comes the experiment. For four weeks, overseen by the obesity specialist Rachel Batterham at UCL, he ate a diet that was 80 per cent UPF, starting each day with bowls of Coco Pops and leaning on frozen ready meals. He put on about 7 kg. His hunger hormone rose and his fullness hormone fell, so he felt hungry soon after eating. Brain scans at the end showed new links between the brain's reward areas and the regions that drive repetitive, automatic behaviour. He also felt more anxious and was badly constipated.

The strongest evidence, he says, comes from a 2019 study by Kevin Hall at the US National Institutes of Health. Volunteers lived in a clinic and ate either an ultra-processed diet or an unprocessed one for two weeks, then switched. The meals were matched for calories, sugar, fat and fibre, and people could eat as much as they liked. On the UPF diet they ate about 500 calories more per day and gained weight. On the unprocessed diet they lost it.

Van Tulleken then clears away the rival explanations. Sugar alone does not account for the obesity rise, and low-carb diets are hard to keep up. Exercise matters for health but barely changes weight, because the body adjusts: Herman Pontzer's work with the Hadza hunter-gatherers of Tanzania found they burn about the same number of calories a day as office workers. Willpower is not the answer either. Weight is strongly shaped by genes and surroundings, and he points to his own identical twin, Xand, who put on weight under the stress of graduate study in Boston even though the two share the same genes.

So what does UPF do to us? He gives several mechanisms. It is soft and "pre-chewed," so we eat it fast, before the body's fullness signals catch up. It is energy-dense and low in fibre. Its flavours and sweeteners send signals that do not match the nutrients that follow, which may confuse the body's ability to regulate what it eats. Some emulsifiers damage the gut lining and its bacteria in animal studies. And in the US, many additives reach the market through a loophole that lets companies declare their own ingredients safe.

The final part is about the industry. Food companies answer to investors who want growth every year, and the cheapest way to grow is to sell more UPF. He shows Nestlé selling products door to door, and even from a floating supermarket, in poor parts of Brazil. He describes the heavy marketing of formula milk to mothers, the environmental cost of producing all this food, and the funding many nutrition scientists and charities receive from food companies.

He ends with advice for readers and for governments. For readers, he borrows from Allen Carr's method for quitting smoking: while reading the book, keep eating the foods you struggle with, but read the ingredient lists and notice what you are eating. For governments, he calls for black warning labels on the front of packs, as Chile has introduced. He also wants limits on marketing, poverty tackled, real food made affordable, and food companies kept out of research and policy.

## Key ideas

**The NOVA classification.** Carlos Monteiro's system sorts food into four groups by degree of processing. Group four, ultra-processed food, is industrially made from refined ingredients and additives.

**A kitchen test.** If a food is wrapped in plastic and has an ingredient you would not keep at home, it is very likely ultra-processed.

**UPF makes us eat more.** In Kevin Hall's controlled study, people ate about 500 extra calories a day on an ultra-processed diet, even with the menus matched for nutrients.

**Not sugar, exercise or willpower.** Van Tulleken argues that none of these explains the obesity rise as well as the spread of UPF. The body compensates for extra exercise, and willpower loses to the food environment.

**Pre-chewed food.** Soft, easy-to-eat textures let us eat quickly and overshoot before the body signals that we are full.

**Mismatched signals.** Flavourings and sweeteners promise nutrients that are not there, which may disrupt the body's normal way of regulating appetite.

**Follow the money.** Food companies are driven by investor demands for growth, and they fund research and lobby governments in ways that protect their products.

## If you read one chapter

The chapter on Kevin Hall's study ("Sure, 'ultra-processed food' sounds bad, but is it really a problem?"), because it is the core evidence the rest of the book stands on.
