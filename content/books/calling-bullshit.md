## In short

Carl Bergstrom, a biologist, and Jevin West, a data scientist, turned their popular University of Washington course into this book. It is a practical guide to the modern kind of nonsense that hides behind numbers, graphs and algorithms, and so looks more convincing than old-fashioned hot air. You don't need advanced maths to catch most of it, they argue, just a few habits of mind and the confidence to ask simple questions.

## Summary

The book begins by pointing out that bullshit is ancient; even animals bluff. The authors define it as language, statistics, charts or other presentation meant to persuade or impress while ignoring the truth. It differs from lying, because the bullshitter doesn't really care whether the claim is true. They lean on the philosopher Harry Frankfurt's essay "On Bullshit" and add a careful look at how technically true statements can still mislead through what they imply. They also introduce Brandolini's principle: refuting bullshit takes far more effort than producing it.

Chapter 2 explains why the problem has grown. The internet made publishing almost free, rewards what gets clicked and shared rather than what is accurate, and lets falsehoods spread faster than corrections.

Chapter 3 begins the toolkit. The authors say you don't need to understand the inner workings of a complicated analysis, which they treat as a black box. Look instead at what goes in and what comes out. If the data going in are biased, or the conclusions coming out don't make sense, the method doesn't matter. Their favourite example is a 2016 paper claiming a machine learning program could detect criminals from their faces. The "criminal" photos were official ID pictures and the "non-criminal" ones were professional photos from the web, so the program had mostly learned to spot who was smiling.

Chapter 4 covers correlation and causation. They show how often news stories turn a correlation into a claim that one thing causes another, and they enjoy spurious correlations, such as the age of Miss America tracking the number of murders by steam and hot objects. Chapter 5 is about numbers. A statistic can be accurate and still mislead through a poor comparison, a percentage of a percentage or a missing denominator. They explain Goodhart's law: once a measure becomes a target, people game it, and it stops measuring what it was meant to.

Chapter 6 deals with selection bias, the many ways the data you see are not a fair sample. They explain the friendship paradox (your friends on average have more friends than you do, because popular people show up in more friend lists) and Berkson's paradox, which can make it look as if attractive people are less kind simply because of whom you are willing to date.

Chapter 7 is on graphs. Bar charts should start at zero, because the eye reads the length of the bar. Their "principle of proportional ink" says the amount of shading should match the size of the number. They name two bad habits: "ducks," where decoration takes over the data, and "glass slippers," where data are squeezed into a format that doesn't fit, such as a fake periodic table.

Chapter 8 turns to big data and artificial intelligence. Algorithms trained on biased data reproduce the bias, and a program that tells wolves from huskies may just be detecting snow in the background. Chapter 9 looks at science, which is self-correcting but still vulnerable. The authors explain p-values and p-hacking, the bias toward publishing positive results, and the prosecutor's fallacy, confusing how likely the evidence is if someone is innocent with how likely they are to be innocent. They also retell the case of Andrew Wakefield's discredited 1998 paper linking vaccines to autism, based on just twelve children.

The last two chapters are practical. To spot bullshit: ask who is telling you and how they know, watch for unfair comparisons, be suspicious of anything too good or too bad to be true, check the size of numbers with quick Fermi estimates, and be most careful with claims that confirm what you already believe. To refute it: use a telling counterexample, an absurd consequence or an analogy, redraw the misleading chart, and stay correct, charitable, clear and willing to admit your own mistakes.

## Key ideas

**Bullshit versus lying.** A liar knows the truth and hides it. A bullshitter doesn't care about the truth at all, only about impressing or persuading.

**Brandolini's principle.** Producing nonsense is cheap, and debunking it takes an order of magnitude more work. That imbalance is why it spreads.

**Treat analyses as black boxes.** You rarely need to understand the method. Check whether the data going in are sound and whether the claims coming out make sense.

**Correlation is not causation.** Two things moving together can reflect chance, a hidden common cause or reverse causation. News reports often ignore this.

**Selection bias.** The data you see are often not a fair sample, which can create patterns that don't exist in the wider world.

**Proportional ink.** In a chart, the amount of ink should match the size of the value it shows. Truncated axes and decorative graphics distort this.

**Goodhart's law.** When a number becomes a target, people optimise for the number instead of the goal behind it.

**Too good to be true.** Claims that seem surprising or that flatter your views deserve the most checking, not the least.

## If you read one chapter

Chapter 10, "Spotting Bullshit," because it collects the book's habits into a short checklist you can use on the next claim you see.
