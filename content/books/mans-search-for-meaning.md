## In short

Viktor Frankl, a Viennese psychiatrist, spent three years in Nazi camps, including Auschwitz and Dachau. The first half of this short book describes what camp life did to the mind and why some prisoners held on when others gave up. The second half introduces logotherapy, his school of psychotherapy, which holds that the main human drive is the search for meaning.

## Summary

Frankl wrote the first version in German in 1946, within a year of liberation. It appeared in English in 1959 and took its present title in 1962. He says at the outset that he is not writing about the great horrors, which others have documented, but about the daily grind of ordinary prisoners and what went on inside their heads.

He was arrested in 1942 with his family and sent first to the Theresienstadt ghetto, then to Auschwitz. On arrival, an SS officer sorted the new prisoners with a small movement of his finger, left or right, and most of those sent one way were dead within hours. Frankl had sewn the manuscript of his first book into his coat lining. It was taken from him with his clothes, and he describes that loss as the moment his old life was erased. His father, mother, brother and wife all died in the camps.

He divides the prisoner's mental life into three phases. The first is shock. New arrivals clung to a "delusion of reprieve," the hope that somehow they would be spared, and many felt a grim curiosity and even humour about their own situation. The second phase, which lasted as long as the imprisonment, was apathy. Hunger, cold and constant beatings deadened the emotions. Prisoners stopped reacting to the sight of others being beaten or dying, and their thoughts narrowed to food and survival.

Even so, Frankl noticed an inner life that the guards could not reach. On a freezing march to a work site at dawn, he found himself talking in his mind with his wife, not knowing whether she was alive, and understood that love could give his life meaning even there. Prisoners called each other over to look at a sunset. Some shared their last piece of bread. These moments convinced him that a person keeps one freedom to the end: the freedom to choose their attitude to what happens to them.

He saw what happened when prisoners lost hope. A fellow inmate dreamed that the war would end on 30 March 1945. When that day approached with no rescue, the man fell ill and died. Frankl also reports that the death rate in his camp rose sharply between Christmas 1944 and New Year, which he put down to prisoners having hoped to be home by Christmas. Those who survived best had something ahead of them: a child waiting, a book to finish. Frankl himself kept going partly by imagining lecturing, after the war, on the psychology of the camps. He quotes Nietzsche's line that someone who has a "why" to live can bear almost any "how."

The third phase was liberation, which brought its own problems. Freed prisoners at first could not feel joy. Some turned bitter or felt entitled to do wrong because wrong had been done to them, and many came home to find no one waiting.

The second part, "Logotherapy in a Nutshell," sets out the theory. Freud stressed the will to pleasure and Adler the will to power, but Frankl argues that people are driven above all by a will to meaning. When they cannot find it, they fall into an "existential vacuum" that shows up as boredom, depression, addiction or aggression. Meaning, he says, can come from three sources: creating a work or doing a deed, experiencing something or loving someone, and the attitude we take towards suffering we cannot avoid. He tells of an elderly doctor who could not get over his wife's death. Frankl asked what would have happened if he had died first. She would have suffered this grief, the man said. His suffering, Frankl pointed out, had spared her that, and seeing it this way helped him bear it.

A postscript added in 1984, "The Case for a Tragic Optimism," argues that one can stay hopeful in the face of pain, guilt and death, what Frankl calls the tragic triad.

## Key ideas

**The last freedom.** Everything can be taken from a person except the freedom to choose how to respond to their circumstances. Frankl saw prisoners exercise it even in Auschwitz.

**Will to meaning.** The deepest human motive is to find meaning, more than pleasure or power. A life that feels meaningless can be miserable even when it is comfortable.

**Three sources of meaning.** You find meaning by doing something worthwhile, by experiencing something or someone, and by the stance you take towards unavoidable suffering.

**Suffering is not meaningless in itself.** Suffering that can be avoided should be avoided. But when it cannot, the way you bear it can become an achievement.

**A reason to live.** Prisoners with a future goal, a person or task waiting for them, were more likely to survive. Losing that future often came before physical collapse.

**Existential vacuum.** Many modern people feel an inner emptiness, which Frankl saw behind boredom, the "Sunday neurosis" of free time, and some addiction and depression.

**Happiness as a side effect.** Chasing success or happiness directly tends to backfire. They follow when you give yourself to a cause or a person.

**Tragic optimism.** You can say yes to life despite pain, guilt and death, by turning suffering into achievement, guilt into change, and the shortness of life into a reason to act.

## If you read one chapter

Part One, "Experiences in a Concentration Camp," because it is the heart of the book, and the scene of the dawn march, where Frankl talks in his mind to his wife, holds its whole idea about love and inner freedom.
