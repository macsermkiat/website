## In short

We praise grit and look down on quitters, but Annie Duke argues that knowing when to stop is just as valuable as knowing when to keep going. Drawing on poker, behavioural economics and business stories, she shows why people stick with losing paths far too long. She then offers practical tools for deciding in advance when you will walk away.

## Summary

Duke opens on Mount Everest in 1996. Climbers set a turnaround time before leaving camp: if you are not on the summit by then, you go down, however close you are. Three clients on Rob Hall's expedition, Stuart Hutchison, John Taske and Lou Kasischke, obeyed the rule and turned back. Hall and his client Doug Hansen kept climbing long past the deadline, reached the top late in the afternoon, and died in the storm that followed. Her point is that grit and quitting are partners. Grit gets you up hard mountains, and quitting stops you from dying on them.

Her second chapter carries the book's central line: quitting on time usually feels like quitting too early. The case study is Stewart Butterfield, whose online game Glitch had fresh funding and a growing user base. He could see that the numbers would never add up, and he shut it down in 2012 while it still looked alive. The small internal chat tool his team had built became Slack, which Salesforce later bought for $27.7 billion.

In Chapter 3, Duke cites an experiment by the economist Steven Levitt, in which people facing a hard choice let a coin flip decide. Those who landed on "quit" and made the change reported being happier months later, which suggests most of us hang on past the point where we should let go. Why do we hang on? The middle of the book walks through the mental traps. Loss aversion makes a loss feel roughly twice as bad as an equal gain feels good, so we avoid turning a paper loss into a real one. Investors show this when they sell winners too early and ride losers down. The organisational psychologist Barry Staw named the pattern "escalation of commitment" in a 1976 paper about the Vietnam War; Duke adds that Staw's own father, Harold, left grocery management to build a discount chain, then kept expanding and held on to losing stores as bigger rivals like Kmart moved in. The sunk cost effect keeps us investing to avoid "wasting" what we already spent. Her big public example is California's high-speed rail line, which voters approved in 2008 and whose cost estimates have since multiplied, while the hardest mountain tunnels remain unbuilt.

Chapter 6 introduces a tool from Astro Teller, head of Google's research lab X. If your plan is to train a monkey to recite Shakespeare while standing on a pedestal, don't start by building the pedestal. Anyone can build a pedestal. Work on the monkey first, because that is where you will discover whether the project is impossible. Pair this with kill criteria, which are signals you decide on in advance that tell you to stop. Good kill criteria combine a state and a date, such as "if we haven't reached this benchmark by June, we end it."

The third part is about identity. We overvalue what we own and what we have built (the endowment and IKEA effects), and we prefer the status quo because a bad result from doing nothing feels less blameworthy than one from acting. Hardest of all is quitting something that defines who we are. Duke contrasts Sears, which sold off profitable businesses like Allstate and Discover to return to its retail roots, with Philips, which sold its famous lighting division to focus on healthcare. Because we are poor judges of our own situation, she recommends a "quitting coach": someone who cares about you but is willing to hurt your feelings. The investor Ron Conway plays this role for founders, agreeing on hard benchmarks with them and then checking back.

The last part turns to opportunity cost. Sticking with one path means missing others. Ants keep sending a few scouts out to explore even when they have found food, and Duke urges readers to do the same. Forced quitting, such as the injury that ended Maya Shankar's violin career and led her into cognitive science, shows that Plan B is sometimes better than Plan A. Finally she warns that fixed goals can blind us. She suggests adding an "unless" to every goal, so that the conditions for stopping are part of the plan from the start.

## Key ideas

**Quitting is a skill, not a failure.** Decisions are made with incomplete information, and quitting is how you act on new information. Used well, it frees time and money for better options.

**On time feels too early.** When you quit at the right moment, things usually still look fine. If you wait until it is obvious, you have almost always waited too long.

**Escalation of commitment.** When a course of action is going badly, people and organisations tend to invest more in it, not less. This happens with stakes large and small.

**Sunk costs.** Money, time and effort already spent are gone whether you continue or not. The real waste is putting more into a path with a poor expected outcome.

**Monkeys and pedestals.** Tackle the hardest, most uncertain part of a project first. Easy progress on the rest can create a false sense that things are going well.

**Kill criteria.** Decide ahead of time what would make you stop, using a measurable state and a deadline. It is easier to set these rules calmly than to judge clearly when you are already in the middle of it.

**A quitting coach.** Find someone who has your long-term interest at heart and permission to tell you hard truths. Outsiders see your situation more clearly than you do.

**Goals need an "unless."** A goal treated as pass or fail can keep you going after it stops making sense. Build in the conditions under which you would change course.

## If you read one chapter

Chapter 6, "Monkeys and Pedestals," because it gives the two most usable tools in the book, working on the hardest problem first and setting kill criteria, with clear examples.
