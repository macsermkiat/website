## In short

A frame is a mental model: a simplified picture of how some part of the world works, which tells us what to pay attention to and what options we have. Kenneth Cukier, a journalist at The Economist, Viktor Mayer-Schönberger, an Oxford professor, and Francis de Véricourt, a business school professor, argue that choosing and changing frames well is the one thing humans do that computers cannot. Good framing rests on three skills, thinking in causes, imagining alternatives and working within constraints, and societies do best when many different frames are allowed to compete.

## Summary

The book opens in early 2020. As Covid-19 spread, most Western countries treated it as something like a bad flu, a disease to be slowed and managed. New Zealand's government under Jacinda Ardern framed it instead as something like SARS, a disease that could be stopped outright. That choice of frame led to closed borders and a hard, early lockdown, and for months the country lived almost virus-free. The authors' point is that the frame came first, and it determined which actions even seemed possible.

They then describe a squeeze on human judgement from two sides. On one side is the machine, the hope that artificial intelligence and data will make decisions for us. On the other is the mob, the populist rejection of reason and evidence. The authors reject both. AI is very good at answering questions, but it can't decide which questions are worth asking.

Their main example is Regina Barzilay at MIT. Her team wanted new antibiotics, and instead of searching for molecules that looked like existing drugs, they trained a system on what actually kills bacteria. In 2020 it turned up a powerful compound they named halicin. The machine did the searching, but the humans framed the search. Frames can also be disastrous: in the Soviet Union, Trofim Lysenko applied communist ideology to biology, insisting that crops of the same "class" would not compete if planted densely, and the results contributed to famine.

The middle of the book covers the three ingredients of framing. The first is causality. People naturally think in causes and effects, which lets us predict what an action will do. Ben Bernanke had studied how the Federal Reserve's mistakes worsened the Great Depression, and in 2008 that causal model guided him to flood the financial system with money. A causal story must also be convincing. Ignaz Semmelweis showed in the 1840s that doctors washing their hands saved mothers' lives, but without germ theory he couldn't explain why, and his colleagues ignored him.

The second is counterfactuals, imagining "what if." We can test options in our heads before acting, which is cheaper and safer than trial and error. The authors stress that useful counterfactuals change only a little of reality at a time; imagining everything differently gives no guidance.

The third is constraints, the limits we place on our imagining. Too few and we are lost. The architect Frank Gehry has said that having no limits made one project harder, and Dr. Seuss wrote Green Eggs and Ham after a bet that he couldn't write a book using only 50 different words. The best constraints are few and can be loosened when needed.

Next comes reframing, for when a frame stops working. Usually the best move is to borrow a frame that exists elsewhere; inventing a new one is rare and risky. Tesla reframed electric cars from worthy compromises into fast, desirable machines. Nokia kept making phones smaller and cheaper while Apple changed the question entirely.

The final chapters widen out to society. Progress needs many frames competing, which the authors call frame pluralism. Their example is the tech industry: in the 1980s, Silicon Valley's open, loosely connected companies pulled ahead of the more hierarchical firms along Boston's Route 128. Teams and countries benefit from people who see problems differently, from tolerance for dissent and from education that teaches people to frame, not just to memorise. The book ends by asking readers to stay vigilant, because the conditions that allow pluralism can be lost to conformity, to machines or to the mob.

## Key ideas

**Frames.** A frame is a mental model that simplifies reality so we can act. Every decision depends on the frame we apply to it, usually without noticing.

**The human advantage.** Computers can answer questions and optimise within a frame, but choosing and changing the frame remains a human skill.

**Causal thinking.** We understand the world by linking causes to effects. Good causal models let us predict the results of actions before we take them.

**Counterfactuals.** Imagining "what if" lets us test options mentally. The most useful ones stay close to reality, changing one thing at a time.

**Constraints.** Limits focus imagination on workable options. Choose a few, and be ready to relax them.

**Reframing.** When a frame fails, first look for one that works in another field. Inventing a completely new frame is powerful but rare.

**Frame pluralism.** Societies and organisations make better decisions when many frames coexist and compete. Uniform thinking is the main threat to good framing.

## If you read one chapter

The chapter on constraints, because the Gehry and Dr. Seuss stories show in a few pages why limits make thinking sharper.
