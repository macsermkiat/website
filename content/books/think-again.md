## In short

We are quick to update our phones and slow to update our beliefs. Adam Grant, a psychologist at the Wharton School, argues that in a fast-changing world the key skill is rethinking: questioning your own assumptions, staying open to other views and changing course when the evidence says so. The book moves from rethinking in your own head, to helping others rethink, to building schools and workplaces where rethinking is normal.

## Summary

The prologue tells the story of the Mann Gulch fire in Montana in 1949. As a wildfire raced toward a crew of smokejumpers, their foreman, Wagner Dodge, did something no one had seen before. He lit a fire in front of him, lay down in the burned patch and let the main blaze pass over him. He survived; most of his crew, who didn't follow him, died. Some were still carrying their heavy tools. Grant uses the image of refusing to drop your tools for the way we cling to familiar ways of thinking even when they are about to kill us.

Part One is about individual rethinking. Chapter 1 describes four mindsets we slip into. As preachers we defend our sacred beliefs, as prosecutors we attack other people's arguments, and as politicians we seek approval. Grant wants us to spend more time as scientists, treating our views as hypotheses to test. BlackBerry's founder, Mike Lazaridis, is his warning: he could not imagine people wanting a phone without a keyboard. In a study of Italian start-ups, founders taught to think like scientists, forming hypotheses and running experiments, earned far more revenue than those who weren't.

Chapter 2 looks at confidence. The Dunning-Kruger effect means people who know little often feel most sure. Grant contrasts this "armchair quarterback" with the impostor who doubts themselves too much. The sweet spot is confident humility: faith in your ability to learn, combined with doubt about your current answers. He tells the story of Halla Tómasdóttir, who started her run for Iceland's presidency in 2016 with almost no support, used her self-doubt as fuel to keep learning, and came a strong second.

Chapter 3 is about the joy of being wrong. Grant describes how Daniel Kahneman reacts with delight when he discovers an error, because it means he has learned something. Separating your identity from your opinions makes it easier; define yourself by your values, not by your views. Chapter 4 argues for productive disagreement. The Wright brothers argued fiercely and often, but about the task, not about each other. Task conflict improves decisions; relationship conflict poisons them.

Part Two turns to persuading others. Chapter 5 starts with a 2019 debate between the champion debater Harish Natarajan and IBM's Project Debater computer. The machine had more facts, but Natarajan won the audience over by acknowledging common ground. Research on expert negotiators points the same way: they ask more questions, present fewer arguments and avoid overwhelming the other side.

Chapter 6 tackles prejudice. Daryl Davis, a Black musician, has spent years befriending members of the Ku Klux Klan and asking them how they can hate him when they don't know him; many have left the Klan. Grant runs experiments with Red Sox and Yankees fans, showing that rivalries soften when people think about how arbitrary their loyalties are. Chapter 7 covers motivational interviewing, a method of asking open questions and helping people find their own reasons to change. A doctor, Arnaud Gagneur, used it with hesitant new parents during a Quebec measles outbreak to raise vaccination rates. Betty Bigombe used patient listening to open talks with the Ugandan rebel leader Joseph Kony.

Part Three moves to groups. Chapter 8 argues that heated debates, on climate change or abortion, improve when people see the issue as complex, with many shades of opinion, instead of two opposing camps. Chapter 9 is about schools. Good teachers let students challenge the textbook and revise their own work, as in Ron Berger's example of a first-grader who drew a butterfly through six drafts based on classmates' feedback. Chapter 10 looks at organisations. NASA's losses of the Challenger and Columbia shuttles came partly from a culture where concerns were dismissed. Grant draws on Amy Edmondson's work on psychological safety and argues for holding people accountable for the quality of their decision process, not just the outcome.

Part Four brings it back to your own life. Chapter 11 warns against tunnel vision in careers and happiness. Picking one career too early, or chasing happiness too hard, can lock us into paths that no longer fit. Grant suggests regular "life checkups" to ask whether you are still where you want to be.

## Key ideas

**Think like a scientist.** Treat your beliefs as hypotheses, not possessions. Test them, and update them when the evidence changes.

**Preacher, prosecutor, politician.** These are the three mindsets that block rethinking: defending your views, attacking others' views and seeking approval.

**Confident humility.** Be secure in your ability to learn while staying unsure about whether your current answer is right.

**The joy of being wrong.** Finding a mistake means you now know more. Tie your identity to values, not to specific opinions.

**Task conflict, not relationship conflict.** Arguing hard about ideas makes groups smarter, as long as it does not turn personal.

**Persuade by listening.** Ask questions, find common ground and offer a few strong reasons rather than many weak ones.

**Complexify.** Presenting an issue as complicated and many-sided makes people more open than framing it as two warring camps.

**Psychological safety.** People speak up about problems only when they can do so without fear of punishment or ridicule.

## If you read one chapter

Chapter 1, because the preacher, prosecutor, politician and scientist give you a quick way to catch your own thinking in the act.
