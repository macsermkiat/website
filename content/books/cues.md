## In short

*Cues: Master the Secret Language of Charismatic Communication* argues that charisma is a set of signals you can learn. Vanessa Van Edwards, who runs the training company Science of People, sorts those signals into four channels (nonverbal, vocal, verbal and imagery, meaning what people see) and shows which ones make you seem warm, which make you seem competent, and which quietly undermine you.

## Summary

The book starts with a pitch that failed. On *Shark Tank* in 2013, Jamie Siminoff presented his video doorbell, then called Doorbot. The product was sound, but he introduced himself with a rising, question-like tone and a lopsided shrug, and none of the investors bought in. The company, renamed Ring, was later sold to Amazon for more than a billion dollars. Van Edwards uses the story to make her main point: people judge us on small cues, often before they have weighed a word we said.

Her formula is that charisma equals warmth plus competence. Warmth answers the question of whether someone is on your side, and competence whether they can deliver. She cites research suggesting these two dimensions account for most of our first impressions. Most people lean heavily toward one: the kind, likeable person who is not taken seriously, or the impressive expert who seems cold. The charismatic few show both, and they can dial one up or down to suit the moment.

Part One covers the body. Charisma cues include leaning in when interested, facing people fully with your feet, torso and head, removing barriers such as a laptop or a crossed arm, and holding purposeful eye contact. Warmth cues include the head tilt, nodding, raising the eyebrows in greeting, a smile that forms slowly, appropriate touch and mirroring the other person. Competence or power cues include standing tall with relaxed shoulders, a slight narrowing of the lower eyelids that signals focus, steepling the fingers, showing open palms and using gestures that illustrate what you are saying. She lists "danger" cues to drop: stepping away, self-soothing touches, blocking the body, shame gestures like rubbing the forehead, and a resting face that looks annoyed. One study she describes found that when Botox stopped people frowning, they felt less anger, a hint that expressions shape feelings as well as show them.

Part Two moves to voice, words and appearance. For a voice that sounds confident, she suggests speaking at the lower end of your comfortable range, ending statements with a downward inflection instead of an upward one, avoiding vocal fry and replacing filler words with pauses. For warmth, greet people with real energy, vary your tone and make small listening noises. She also suggests borrowing the vocal style of a speaker you admire.

The verbal chapter treats word choice as a cue in its own right. She has readers audit their emails and texts for warmth words and competence words, rewrite stale openers like "Hope you're well", and match the vocabulary of the people they are talking to. Some people want data, others want a story, and you can learn to hear which. An example she likes is Hotmail's early growth, driven largely by a line added to the bottom of every outgoing email. She notes that a postscript is one of the most-read parts of an email.

The imagery chapter covers what people take from your appearance and surroundings: clothes, the background on a video call, the objects on your desk and the colours you use. Red tends to prompt action, blue suggests calm and trust, and yellow reads as cheerful but can be tiring. A marketing agency she describes won a client by pitching in that client's own colours and fonts.

Throughout, Van Edwards frames the skill as working in both directions. Once you know the cues, you can read other people more accurately and choose which signals you send, so that how you come across matches what you mean.

## Key ideas

**Charisma is warmth plus competence.** People want to know whether you mean well and whether you are capable. Charisma is showing both, and most people need to raise whichever one they neglect.

**Four channels.** Every interaction sends signals through your body, your voice, your words and your imagery, meaning your appearance and what people see around you. People read all four, mostly without noticing.

**Warmth cues.** Head tilts, nods, raised eyebrows, slow smiles and mirroring tell people you are friendly and listening.

**Competence cues.** Upright posture, steady eye contact, open palms, steepled fingers and explanatory gestures make you seem capable and in control.

**Question inflection hurts.** Ending a statement with a rising tone makes it sound unsure. A falling tone at the end of a sentence makes the same words sound confident.

**Drop the danger cues.** Distancing, self-soothing, blocking and a bothered resting face can cancel out everything else you do right.

**Match your audience.** Echo the words, pace and preferences of the people you are speaking to, whether they want warmth, data or a story.

## If you read one chapter

The opening chapter on warmth and competence, because once you have that frame you start seeing it in every meeting and every pitch.
