## In short

Charlie Munger was Warren Buffett's partner at Berkshire Hathaway for more than four decades, and this book collects eleven of his talks, edited by Peter Kaufman, with chapters on his life and habits of mind. His central idea is that you make better decisions by carrying around the big ideas from many disciplines, a "latticework of mental models," and by working hard to avoid stupidity rather than trying to be brilliant. The title nods to Benjamin Franklin's Poor Richard's Almanack, and Franklin is Munger's model throughout.

## Summary

The book opens with a foreword by Buffett and then a portrait of Munger. Born in Omaha in 1924, he worked as a boy in the grocery store owned by Buffett's grandfather, studied meteorology at Caltech for the Army Air Corps during the war, and got into Harvard Law School without an undergraduate degree. He practised law in Los Angeles, co-founded the firm Munger, Tolles & Olson, and ran his own investment partnership before joining Berkshire as vice chairman. The portrait also covers hard times, including the death of his young son from leukaemia and the loss of an eye after surgery.

The next chapters set out his approach. Munger reads constantly across fields, sorts decisions into "yes," "no" and "too hard," and waits patiently for rare, obvious opportunities. He looks for businesses with durable advantages, bought at sensible prices, and holds them a long time. A chapter of short sayings, the "Mungerisms," follows.

Then come the talks, roughly in the order he gave them. The first, a 1986 commencement speech at the Harvard School in Los Angeles, turns advice on its head. Instead of telling graduates how to be happy, Munger lists sure ways to be miserable: be unreliable, learn only from your own experience, stay down after your first big setback, and never think backwards about what to avoid. The method behind the joke is inversion, from the mathematician Carl Jacobi's advice to "invert, always invert."

The 1994 talk at USC's business school is the core of the book. Munger argues that worldly wisdom comes from knowing the main models of many fields, such as compound interest from maths, redundancy from engineering, evolution from biology, scale advantages from economics, and the quirks of human psychology. If you know only one discipline, you are like the man with a hammer to whom every problem looks like a nail. He applies this to investing: markets work like the betting pool at a racetrack, so the trick is to bet rarely, and heavily, when the odds are clearly mispriced.

In 1996 he gave a thought experiment about Coca-Cola. Imagine it is 1884 in Atlanta and you must turn $2 million into a drinks business worth $2 trillion by 2034. Munger reasons his way to the answer using basic arithmetic, Pavlovian conditioning, brand names and scale, showing how ordinary models combine to explain an extraordinary result.

Several talks deal with professions and institutions. He tells Harvard Law graduates that professionals need broad, multidisciplinary training and checklist habits like airline pilots. He criticises charitable foundations for paying layers of consultants and fund managers. A fable written in 2000, "The Great Financial Scandal of 2003," imagines a company that games stock-option accounting until it collapses; the Enron scandal followed soon after. A 2003 lecture at UC Santa Barbara takes academic economics to task for "physics envy," for staying inside its own boundaries and for ignoring psychology.

Later editions add the 2007 USC law commencement, his gentlest talk. The best way to get what you want, he says, is to deserve it. Keep learning all your life, as Buffett does; avoid envy, resentment and self-pity; and be reliable.

The final talk, "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment," revised in 2005, lists 25 tendencies that lead people astray. The first is the power of incentives. When FedEx could not get its night shift to finish on time, it stopped paying workers by the hour and paid them by the shift, and the problem vanished. Others include social proof, envy, denial, excessive self-regard, reciprocation and the overweighting of whatever comes to mind easily. His last and most important tendency is the "lollapalooza," when several biases push in the same direction at once and produce extreme behaviour.

## Key ideas

**A latticework of mental models.** Learn the big ideas from many fields and connect them. Real problems cross disciplines, and one model alone will mislead you.

**Invert, always invert.** Think about a problem backwards. Ask what would guarantee failure, then avoid those things.

**Avoid stupidity before seeking brilliance.** Much of long-term success comes from not making big, avoidable mistakes. Consistently not being stupid is easier than being very smart.

**Circle of competence.** Know the edges of what you understand and stay inside them. Put hard cases in the "too hard" pile.

**Few big bets.** Wait for the rare opportunity where the odds are clearly in your favour, then act decisively. Most of the time, do nothing.

**Incentives rule behaviour.** People respond powerfully to how they are rewarded. If you want to understand or change what people do, look at their incentives first.

**Lollapalooza effects.** When several psychological tendencies act together, the result can be far larger than any one of them alone, as in auctions, cults and financial manias.

**Deserve what you want.** Munger's personal ethic is to be reliable, keep learning and avoid envy and self-pity. He treats good character as practical, not merely admirable.

## If you read one chapter

Talk Eleven, "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment," because its list of 25 tendencies is the most complete and usable part of Munger's thinking.
