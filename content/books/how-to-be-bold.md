## In short

Ranjay Gulati, who teaches leadership at Harvard Business School, set out to learn why some people act boldly under uncertainty while others freeze. His answer is that courageous people are not fearless. They have ways of thinking that keep fear from taking over, and those ways can be learned. He organises them into nine "C's," six for individuals and three for groups.

## Summary

The book opens with a scene from Gulati's childhood. His mother once had a frightening encounter with a property developer and a gun. Afterwards she told her son that she had been scared, but being scared was no reason to do nothing. That, in short, is the book's definition of courage: taking action in the face of fear, usually for something that matters.

Gulati starts from the brain. Fear is our default response to uncertainty and loss of control, because caution kept our ancestors alive. Following Aristotle, he places courage between cowardice and recklessness. Real boldness is thoughtful. Frances Haugen spent many months deciding whether to blow the whistle on Facebook, while Captain Chesley Sullenberger had seconds to decide to land his plane on the Hudson River. Both were brave, but neither was careless.

The first six C's make up the inner game. Coping means telling yourself a steadying story about who you are and what you are doing. Gulati mentions a Ukrainian mergers lawyer who became a commando because staying on the sidelines felt unbearable. Confidence comes in two kinds: skill in your own field, and a broader sense that you can handle the unknown. He describes Albert Bandura's studies of people terrified of snakes, who were led step by step to touch one. Afterwards they felt more capable in other parts of their lives too.

Commitment is about purpose and the beliefs you will not give up. Alexei Navalny's return to Russia, knowing he would be arrested, is his example of conviction that ignores cost-benefit sums. Connection is the support that makes risk bearable: encouragement, information, practical help and honest feedback. Haugen, he points out, had her mother, lawyers, journalists and clergy behind her.

Comprehension is about acting when you do not have the facts. Drawing on the organisational psychologist Karl Weick, Gulati says you often have to act your way into knowing. His central story comes from the Fukushima Daini nuclear plant in 2011, about ten kilometres from the Daiichi plant that melted down. With power and sensors knocked out by the tsunami, the plant manager, Naohiro Masuda, wrote on a whiteboard what his team knew and what they needed to find out. He sent volunteers out to gather information, updated the board, and repeated the cycle until the reactors could be cooled.

Calm is about controlling emotion under pressure. Sullenberger ran through a checklist. Adele, who has stage fright, has a pre-show ritual. Simone Biles stayed off social media while she recovered. Gulati also recommends reframing a threat as a challenge and focusing on the next step of the process rather than the outcome.

The last three C's are the outer game, about groups. Clan is the shared identity that makes people take risks for each other, like the Marine Corps with its motto "Semper Fidelis." Charisma, he argues, is less a gift than a set of behaviours leaders can learn, and it lets them lend others their nerve. Culture is what makes boldness normal in an organisation. Netflix, which encouraged dissent and tolerated failure as it moved from mailing DVDs to streaming, is contrasted with Blockbuster, which played it safe and disappeared.

Gulati closes with leaders who chose the hard job. Mike Mahoney took over Boston Scientific after a disastrous acquisition had shrunk its value, against advice, and rebuilt it into a company worth well over a hundred billion dollars. Anne Mulcahy took charge of Xerox when it was close to bankruptcy. His research on firms in recessions points the same way: the few companies that came out stronger kept investing while others only cut costs. Playing it safe, he warns, can leave you with a life that is safe but incomplete.

## Key ideas

**Courage is not fearlessness.** Brave people feel fear like everyone else. What sets them apart is that they act anyway.

**Courage can be learned.** Boldness is a set of habits of mind that grow with practice, like a muscle, rather than a fixed personality trait.

**Smart courage.** True courage is deliberate and aimed at a worthwhile goal. Acting without thinking is recklessness.

**Act your way into knowing.** When the situation is unclear, take small steps, learn from what happens and adjust, as the Fukushima Daini team did with its whiteboard.

**Calm through ritual and focus.** Checklists, routines, reframing and attention to the next task keep fear from hijacking your judgement.

**Courage is never solo.** Emotional, practical and informational support from others makes bold choices possible and easier to sustain.

**Collective courage.** Leaders can build shared identity and cultures where speaking up and experimenting are expected, so boldness spreads through a group.

**The cost of playing safe.** Doing nothing is also a risk. Companies and people who only protect what they have tend to fall behind.

## If you read one chapter

The chapter on comprehension, because the story of the Fukushima Daini whiteboard is the clearest picture in the book of what calm, step-by-step courage looks like.
