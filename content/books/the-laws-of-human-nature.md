## In short

Robert Greene, the author of *The 48 Laws of Power*, argues that we are all driven by the same set of impulses that evolution built into us: vanity, envy, the pull of the group, the fear of death. Most of us deny this about ourselves while seeing it clearly in others. The book has eighteen "laws," each built around a long historical story, and its aim is to make you a calmer observer of people, starting with yourself.

## Summary

Greene's starting point is that people believe they are in control of what they do, and mostly they are not. Our behaviour follows patterns laid down over millions of years of living in groups. You can either be pushed around by those patterns or learn to see them. The book is a manual for seeing them.

The first law is irrationality. Greene tells the story of Pericles, who led Athens through calm, patient judgement and tried to keep his city's emotions in check. After he died of the plague, the Athenians gave in to fear and ambition and launched the disastrous expedition to Sicily. Greene's advice is to notice when a feeling is making the decision for you and to cultivate a cooler, wider view of events.

Next comes narcissism. Everyone is self-absorbed to some degree. Greene contrasts deep narcissists such as Stalin, who need constant attention and turn on anyone who withholds it, with healthy ones such as the explorer Ernest Shackleton, who kept his crew alive on the Antarctic ice by paying close attention to their moods. Self-love, he says, can be turned outward into empathy with practice.

Several chapters teach you to read people. Milton Erickson, the psychiatrist who was paralysed by polio as a teenager, learned to read his family's body language while lying in bed, and Greene uses him to show that people's masks slip in their tone, posture and small gestures. The chapter on compulsive behaviour follows Howard Hughes, whose early flashes of brilliance hid a character that later wrecked his projects. The lesson is that character shows in repeated patterns, and people rarely change them.

Then Greene turns to desire and perspective. Coco Chanel made herself and her brand desirable by staying elusive. The South Sea Bubble of 1720, which ruined even clever investors, shows how short-term excitement blinds us to long-term consequences. Lyndon Johnson, who rose in the Senate by flattering its powerful men, illustrates the law of defensiveness: people cling to a good opinion of themselves, so you win them over by confirming it rather than attacking it.

The middle of the book goes inward. Anton Chekhov, raised by a brutal father, chose to forgive him and to see the world more generously, and Greene uses him to argue that your attitude shapes what happens to you. Richard Nixon shows what happens when the dark side of a personality, his "shadow," is denied and then leaks out. The chapter on envy follows Mary Shelley and her friend Jane Williams, whose warmth hid resentment. Michael Eisner's later years at Disney show grandiosity, where success convinces a leader that he cannot fail.

The last third widens to groups and history. Caterina Sforza, a Renaissance ruler who was both fierce and seductive, is Greene's example of someone who drew on both masculine and feminine traits. Martin Luther King Jr. shows how a strong sense of purpose steadies a life. Gao Yuan's memoir of his school during China's Cultural Revolution shows how groups drag decent people into cruelty. Elizabeth I, who kept loyalty by putting her country's interests before her own vanity, is the model leader. John D. Rockefeller illustrates aggression hidden behind a pleasant manner, and the French Revolution shows how each generation rebels against the one before.

The book ends with Flannery O'Connor, diagnosed with lupus in her twenties, who used the knowledge that she would die young to write with focus and to feel closer to other people. Greene closes by saying that facing our own mortality is what finally frees us to live fully.

## Key ideas

**We are less rational than we think.** Emotions make most of our decisions and reason arrives afterwards to justify them. The first step is to notice this in the moment.

**Healthy narcissism.** Everyone needs self-esteem, but it can either make you demand attention or be turned outward. Turning it outward means listening closely and trying to feel your way into another person's view.

**Character is pattern.** How someone behaved in the past, especially under stress, is the best guide to how they will behave again. Charm and promises matter less than repeated actions.

**Confirm people's self-opinion.** People defend their image of themselves as good, smart and independent. If you want to persuade them, work with that image instead of against it.

**The shadow.** The traits we hide and deny do not go away. They come out under pressure, often in exaggerated form, so it is better to know your own.

**Envy hides behind friendliness.** Envy is shameful, so people disguise it, often as over-eager praise or sudden closeness. Watch for it, and keep your own envy in check by admiring instead of comparing.

**The pull of the group.** In a group, people imitate each other and lose their own judgement. Stay part of the group while keeping a private, independent mind.

**Facing death.** Accepting that life is short sharpens your sense of purpose and makes you more patient with others, who share the same fate.

## If you read one chapter

Chapter 3, "See Through People's Masks," because Milton Erickson's story is memorable and its advice on reading non-verbal cues is the most practical in the book.
