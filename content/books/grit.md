## In short

Angela Duckworth, a psychologist at the University of Pennsylvania, found that the people who reach the top of hard fields are not the most talented but the ones who keep working towards one goal for years. She calls this combination of passion and perseverance "grit." The book explains why it matters, how to measure it, and how people can grow it in themselves and in others.

## Summary

Duckworth begins with her father, who often told her she was no genius. After working as a management consultant and then teaching maths in public schools, she noticed that her best students were not always the quickest. That hunch led her into psychology and to her first big study, at the US Military Academy at West Point.

Every summer, new West Point cadets go through seven weeks of brutal basic training called Beast Barracks, and some quit. The academy's own measure of applicants, the Whole Candidate Score, which combines grades, fitness and leadership, did a poor job of predicting who would drop out. A short questionnaire Duckworth wrote, the Grit Scale, did much better. She found similar results with salespeople who kept their jobs, students who finished high school in Chicago, and children who went furthest in the National Spelling Bee.

Chapter 2 argues that we are "distracted by talent." We love the idea of natural gifts, so we overlook the years of effort behind performances that look effortless. Chapter 3 gives her main formula. Talent times effort gives you skill; skill times effort gives you achievement. Effort appears twice, which is why someone less gifted who keeps working can overtake someone brilliant who stops.

Chapter 4 includes the Grit Scale so you can score yourself. It also describes grit as having a top-level goal that organises all the smaller ones. Duckworth tells the story, attributed to Warren Buffett, of writing down 25 career goals, circling the top five, and treating the other twenty as things to avoid. Gritty people, she says, are stubborn about their main goal but flexible about how to reach it. Chapter 5 adds good news: grit tends to rise with age, and it can be learned.

Part II covers four things gritty people develop from within. The first is interest. Passion rarely arrives in a flash; it usually starts with light curiosity that grows as you learn more. The second is practice, drawing on Anders Ericsson's research on deliberate practice: setting a stretch goal, working on your weaknesses with full focus, and getting quick feedback. Duckworth found that spelling bee finalists who spent more time on this tedious solo study did better than those who just read for fun or quizzed each other.

The third is purpose, the sense that your work matters to other people. She uses the parable of three bricklayers: one is laying bricks, one is building a church, and one is building a house of God. The fourth is hope, meaning the belief that your own efforts can improve things. Here she draws on Martin Seligman's work on learned helplessness and Carol Dweck's research on the growth mindset.

Part III looks at how grit is shaped from outside. Duckworth recommends what she calls wise parenting, which is both warm and demanding, as opposed to permissive or authoritarian. In her own home the family follows the Hard Thing Rule: everyone does something difficult that requires practice, you can't quit until a natural stopping point such as the end of a season, and you choose your own hard thing. She reports research showing that students who stuck with extracurricular activities for several years, rather than hopping between them, did better later in life.

She also describes cultures that build grit, including the Seattle Seahawks under coach Pete Carroll and West Point itself. When people join a group with high standards, they tend to rise to match them. She ends by saying that grit is not the only thing that matters, but that sticking with something you love, and loving it for a long time, is within most people's reach.

## Key ideas

**Grit.** Grit is passion and perseverance for very long-term goals. It means staying with one direction for years, not just working hard for a week.

**Effort counts twice.** Effort turns talent into skill, and then turns skill into achievement. A modest talent with steady effort often beats a large talent that coasts.

**The talent bias.** We tend to admire natural ability and underrate hard work, which can lead us to write off people too early.

**Goal hierarchy.** Keep one top-level goal and let lower goals serve it. Be firm about the destination and willing to change the route.

**Interest comes first.** Passion usually develops slowly from exploring and discovering, not from a sudden revelation.

**Deliberate practice.** Real improvement comes from focused work on specific weaknesses with immediate feedback. It is effortful and often not fun.

**Purpose and hope.** Grit is sustained by believing your work helps others and by trusting that your own efforts can make things better.

**The Hard Thing Rule.** A family rule for building grit: take on something hard, finish what you start, and pick it yourself.

## If you read one chapter

Chapter 3, "Effort Counts Twice," because its two simple equations capture the book's whole argument about talent and work.
