## In short

Strogatz, a mathematician at Cornell, tells the story of calculus as the story of one trick, which he calls the Infinity Principle: cut a hard, curved, changing problem into infinitely many easy straight pieces, solve each, and reassemble them. The book moves in roughly historical order from ancient Greece to Newton and Leibniz, then shows calculus at work in things like GPS, CT scans, microwave ovens and HIV treatment. You need no maths beyond school algebra to follow it.

## Summary

Strogatz opens with a line Richard Feynman once said to the novelist Herman Wouk: calculus is the language God talks. He wants to show why. His claim is that calculus rests on a single strategy. Curved shapes and changing motion are hard. Straight lines and constant speeds are easy. So you chop the hard thing into infinitely many tiny pieces, each nearly straight or nearly constant, deal with those, and put the answers back together.

Chapter 1, "Infinity," shows the idea with a pizza. Cut a circle into many thin slices and lay them out alternately pointing up and down. The result looks like a wavy rectangle, and with infinitely many slices it becomes a true rectangle with height equal to the radius and width equal to half the circumference. That gives the area of a circle, pi times the radius squared. Strogatz also brings in Zeno's paradoxes, such as Achilles never catching the tortoise, to show how unsettling infinity was to the Greeks.

Chapter 2 belongs to Archimedes. He trapped pi between polygons with 96 sides inside and outside a circle, showing it lies between 3 10/71 and 3 1/7. He found the area under a parabola by filling it with ever smaller triangles. His methods were the first real use of the Infinity Principle, nearly two thousand years before calculus had a name.

Chapter 3 moves to the laws of motion. Galileo rolled balls down ramps and found that the distances covered in successive equal times go up as the odd numbers 1, 3, 5, 7, so total distance grows with the square of time. Kepler, working from Tycho Brahe's data, found that planets move in ellipses and sweep out equal areas in equal times. Both discoveries cried out for a mathematics of change.

Chapters 4 and 5 bring in that mathematics. Fermat and Descartes joined algebra and geometry so that curves could be written as equations. Fermat found a way to locate the highest and lowest points of a curve, and used a similar idea to explain why light bends when it enters water: it takes the quickest path. With graphs of functions such as powers, exponentials and logarithms, the stage was set.

Chapters 6 and 7 are the core. A derivative is a rate of change, like the speed shown on a car's speedometer at one instant. An integral adds up an accumulation, like distance built up from speed. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, found independently by Newton and Leibniz in the second half of the 1600s, says these are opposites. Newton worked much of it out while at home during the plague of 1665 and 1666, using infinite series as his main tool. Leibniz developed the notation, dx and the long S of the integral sign, that we still use. The two men later fell into a bitter dispute over who came first.

Chapter 8, "Fictions of the Mind," looks at infinitesimals, the infinitely small quantities that made calculus work but that nobody could justify for more than a century. It then jumps to the 1990s, when Alan Perelson and David Ho used a simple differential equation to show that HIV was being produced and cleared in the body far faster than anyone thought, a result that helped support combination drug therapy.

The last chapters show the payoff. Newton's laws turned the solar system into a set of equations, and centuries later Katherine Johnson used the same mathematics to compute spacecraft paths at NASA. Joseph Fourier showed that any shape of wave can be built from sine waves, an idea behind microwave ovens, music compression and the CT scanner. Strogatz closes with the future: chaos and nonlinear systems, where equations exist but have no neat solutions, and the growing role of computers, including game-playing AI like AlphaZero. He ends with how well calculus-based theories predict nature, from the positron to gravitational waves.

## Key ideas

**The Infinity Principle.** Break a complicated problem into infinitely many simple parts, solve those, and recombine them. Strogatz argues that this one move is the heart of calculus.

**Derivatives measure change.** A derivative tells you how fast something is changing at a single instant, such as a car's speed at the moment you glance at the speedometer.

**Integrals measure accumulation.** An integral adds up infinitely many tiny contributions, such as the total distance travelled from a record of changing speed.

**The Fundamental Theorem.** Finding rates and adding up accumulations are inverse operations. Newton and Leibniz each saw this, and it turned calculus into a practical toolkit.

**Infinity is useful but tricky.** Zeno's paradoxes and the doubts over infinitesimals show that infinity can produce nonsense if handled carelessly. Calculus works by approaching infinity as a limit.

**Fourier's idea.** Any wave or signal can be written as a sum of simple sine waves. Much of modern technology, from medical scanners to audio files, depends on this.

**Calculus runs the modern world.** GPS, space flight, epidemic models and drug treatments all rest on differential equations, often in ways their users never see.

## If you read one chapter

Chapter 2, "The Man Who Harnessed Infinity," because Archimedes' work with polygons and parabolas makes the Infinity Principle concrete and easy to picture.
