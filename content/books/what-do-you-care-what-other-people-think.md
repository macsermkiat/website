## In short

Like "Surely You're Joking", this book grew out of Feynman's taped conversations with Ralph Leighton, and it came out in 1988, the year Feynman died. The first half collects stories about the two people who shaped him early, his father and his first wife Arline, plus odds and ends from his travels. The second half is his own account of serving on the commission that investigated the 1986 Challenger explosion, and of how he found and showed the cause.

## Summary

The first part, "A Curious Character", opens with Feynman's father, Melville, a uniform salesman with no scientific training who taught his son how to think. When they read the encyclopedia together, he would turn a dinosaur's size into a picture: tall enough to put its head through their upstairs window. On walks he pointed out that you can know a bird's name in every language and still know nothing about the bird; what matters is watching what it does. When young Richard asked why a ball rolls to the back of his wagon when he pulls it forward, his father said the tendency is called inertia, but nobody really knows why it happens. Feynman credits these lessons for his lifelong habit of separating a name from an understanding.

The title story is about Arline Greenbaum, his high school sweetheart. She fell ill, and after a long confusion about the diagnosis it turned out to be tuberculosis, which was then usually fatal. Feynman married her anyway, against his family's worries, and when he went to work at Los Alamos she lived in a sanatorium in Albuquerque, where he visited on weekends. She was playful and unembarrassed. She sent him a box of pencils printed with a loving message, and when he scraped the words off so colleagues wouldn't see, she asked him the question that gives the book its name. She died in June 1945. Feynman describes his odd numbness, finding a rational explanation for a stopped clock in her room, and breaking down only weeks later when he saw a dress in a shop window that she would have liked.

The rest of the first part is lighter. In one experiment he learns he can count in his head while reading but not while talking, and his colleague John Tukey finds the opposite, because each of them counts in a different way. There are trips to Japan, letters home from conferences, and some of the drawings he made after learning to draw in his forties.

The second part, "Mr. Feynman Goes to Washington", is the bulk of the book. On 28 January 1986 the shuttle Challenger broke apart shortly after launch, killing all seven crew. NASA's acting head, William Graham, a former student of Feynman's, asked him to join the presidential commission led by William Rogers. Feynman, already ill with cancer, reluctantly agreed. He soon found the formal hearings too slow, and went off on his own to talk to engineers at NASA centres and contractors.

A hint from a fellow commissioner, Air Force General Donald Kutyna, pointed him toward cold weather. The launch morning had been freezing, and the rubber O-rings sealing the joints of the solid rocket boosters had shown signs of damage on earlier flights. At a televised hearing Feynman squeezed a piece of O-ring rubber in a small clamp, dropped it in his glass of ice water, and showed that when he released it the rubber did not spring back. Cold rubber could not seal the joint fast enough.

He then dug into NASA's culture. Engineers he spoke to put the chance of losing a shuttle at around 1 in 100. Managers claimed about 1 in 100,000, which would mean launching every day for three hundred years and losing just one. Earlier damage to the O-rings had been treated as acceptable because nothing had gone wrong yet. Feynman concluded that managers had promised Congress more than the shuttle could deliver and stopped listening to engineers who said otherwise. The commission's report was reluctant to include his blunt findings, and after a fight they went in as an appendix, Appendix F, which ends by saying that for a technology to succeed, reality must come before public relations, because "nature cannot be fooled".

The book closes with "The Value of Science", a talk from 1955 on why scientific knowledge and the freedom to doubt are worth defending.

## Key ideas

**A name is not knowledge.** His father's bird lesson: knowing what something is called tells you nothing about how it works. Understanding comes from watching and asking why.

**Honest ignorance.** Saying "nobody knows why", as his father did about inertia, is better than a confident but empty answer. Feynman treats admitting ignorance as the start of science.

**Don't live by other people's judgment.** Arline's question, what do you care what other people think, was about pencils, but it became a rule for him: do what you think is right and interesting, and ignore how it looks.

**Go to the people who do the work.** On the commission Feynman got his best information by skipping the managers and talking to engineers and technicians directly.

**Simple demonstrations beat long arguments.** The ice-water test took a minute and made the cause of the disaster clear to everyone watching on television.

**Warnings treated as safety margins.** NASA had seen O-ring damage before and called it acceptable because flights kept succeeding. Feynman argued that damage the design never expected is a sign that something is wrong.

**Reality before public relations.** When an organisation's promises outrun what its engineers know, the gap shows up as a huge difference in risk estimates. Nature does not care about the promises.

## If you read one chapter

Read the title story about Arline, because it shows a side of Feynman the other books only hint at, and explains the question he lived by.
