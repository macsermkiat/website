## In short

Daniel Kahneman sums up decades of research, much of it with his late collaborator Amos Tversky, on how people actually make judgements and choices. He describes two systems in the mind: System 1, which is fast, automatic and intuitive, and System 2, which is slow, effortful and logical. System 1 does most of the work and is usually right, but it makes systematic mistakes, and the book is a tour of those mistakes and what, if anything, we can do about them.

## Summary

Part One introduces the two characters. System 1 is what tells you that 2 + 2 = 4, reads anger on a face and drives a car on an empty road. System 2 is what you use to work out 17 × 24 or fill in a tax form. System 2 is lazy: it tends to accept whatever System 1 suggests. Try this: a bat and a ball cost $1.10, and the bat costs $1 more than the ball. How much is the ball? Most people say 10 cents. The right answer is 5 cents. System 1 jumps to conclusions using only what is in front of it, a habit Kahneman calls WYSIATI, "what you see is all there is." When faced with a hard question, it often quietly answers an easier one instead. (Kahneman later admitted that some of the priming studies in this part have not held up.)

Part Two covers heuristics and biases, the mental shortcuts that produce errors. Anchoring: people who spun a wheel rigged to stop at 10 or 65 gave very different estimates of the share of African nations in the United Nations, though the wheel was irrelevant. Availability: we judge how common something is by how easily examples come to mind, so vivid events like plane crashes feel more likely than they are. Representativeness: in the famous Linda problem, people read about a bright, politically active woman and rank "feminist bank teller" as more likely than "bank teller," which is logically impossible. We also neglect base rates and fall for the law of small numbers, reading patterns into tiny samples.

A favourite story is about regression to the mean. An Israeli flight instructor told Kahneman that praise made cadets worse and shouting made them better. In fact, an unusually good landing is likely to be followed by a more ordinary one, and a bad one by a better one, whatever the instructor says.

Part Three is about overconfidence. We build tidy stories about the past and so believe the future is more predictable than it is. Kahneman describes assessing officer candidates in the Israeli army; his team felt sure of their ratings, even after learning that the ratings barely predicted success. Simple formulas often beat expert judgement, as the psychologist Paul Meehl showed and as the Apgar score for newborns demonstrates. Expert intuition can be trusted only in environments that are regular enough to learn and where feedback is quick, as with chess players or firefighters. He also tells how his own team, writing a school textbook in Israel, expected to finish in two years; the outside view, how long similar projects had taken, suggested seven to ten years, and it took eight. This is the planning fallacy. As a remedy he recommends Gary Klein's premortem: imagine the project has failed, and write the story of why.

Part Four presents prospect theory, the work that won Kahneman the 2002 Nobel Prize in economics. Standard economics assumes people judge outcomes by final wealth. In fact we judge gains and losses relative to a reference point, and losses loom larger: most people will not accept a coin flip that risks losing $100 unless they could win about $200. This loss aversion explains the endowment effect, why we value what we own more than what we don't. The fourfold pattern shows when we take risks and when we avoid them, which explains both lottery tickets and insurance. And framing matters: the same choice described in terms of lives saved or lives lost gets different answers.

Part Five distinguishes the experiencing self, who lives through each moment, from the remembering self, who keeps score. In a study of colonoscopies, patients' memories of pain depended on the worst moment and the final moment, not on how long it lasted. In a lab experiment, people chose to repeat a longer episode of holding a hand in cold water, because it ended slightly less painfully. The remembering self makes our decisions, even though the experiencing self does the living. Kahneman closes with the focusing illusion, which he sums up in one line: "Nothing in life is as important as you think it is when you are thinking about it."

## Key ideas

**System 1 and System 2.** Fast intuitive thinking handles most of life; slow deliberate thinking is effortful and lazy, so it often signs off on intuition's mistakes.

**WYSIATI.** "What you see is all there is." We build confident stories from whatever information is at hand and rarely ask what is missing.

**Substitution.** When a question is hard, the mind answers an easier related one without noticing.

**Anchoring.** An initial number, even an irrelevant one, pulls later estimates toward it.

**Regression to the mean.** Extreme results tend to be followed by more ordinary ones. We invent causes, like praise or punishment, for what is really chance.

**The planning fallacy and the outside view.** We underestimate time and cost by focusing on our own plan. Looking at how similar projects turned out gives a better forecast.

**Loss aversion.** Losses weigh about twice as much as equal gains, which shapes how we bargain, invest and hold on to things.

**The two selves.** Memory judges experiences by their peak and their ending, and neglects how long they lasted. Our choices follow memory, not experience.

## If you read one chapter

Chapter 26, "Prospect Theory," because it is the Nobel-winning core of the book and explains loss aversion in a few pages.
