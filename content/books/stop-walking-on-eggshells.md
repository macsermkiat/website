## In short

*Stop Walking on Eggshells* is written for the people around someone with borderline personality disorder (BPD): the ones who never know which version of their partner, parent or child will walk through the door. Paul T. Mason, a psychotherapist, and Randi Kreger, a writer who has spent decades collecting families' stories, explain the disorder and then shift the focus to the reader. You can't cure the other person, they argue, but you can change how you respond, set limits, stay safe and decide what you want. The book first appeared in 1998, and the third edition (2020) adds material on narcissism, men with BPD and children.

## Summary

The title names the feeling the book is for. Readers are asked whether someone close to them flies into rages out of proportion to the trigger, blames them for everything, acts charming in public and cruel at home, or makes demands that can never quite be met. Then they are asked whether they feel exhausted, confused and constantly braced for the next blow. Short first-person stories follow: Jon, whose wife adored him one day and despised him the next; Mary, raising a son who hurt himself; Kendra, criticised relentlessly by her mother; Ally, who in the end cut off contact with her sister.

Next comes the disorder itself. BPD is diagnosed when at least five of nine traits show up across a person's life: frantic efforts to avoid abandonment, unstable and intense relationships, a shaky sense of identity, harmful impulsiveness, self-harm or suicidal behaviour, fast mood swings, chronic emptiness, intense anger, and paranoia or dissociation under stress. The authors explain the patterns behind these. In splitting, people are either wonderful or terrible, with nothing in between, and the verdict can flip after a single conversation. Projection means the person's own unbearable feelings get attributed to you. Feelings come to stand in for facts: if they feel betrayed, you must have betrayed them. The authors return again and again to what drives this: pain, and a fear of being left.

The third edition makes a distinction that runs through the whole book. "Conventional" BPD is the kind seen in clinics: people who suffer openly, harm themselves and seek help. The "unconventional" kind is, the authors argue, far more common. These people often function well at work, deny that anything is wrong, and push their distress outward onto the people close to them. That second group rarely appears in research, yet it is the one most readers are living with. A new chapter covers narcissistic personality disorder, since the authors cite a study finding that nearly 40 percent of people with BPD also have narcissistic personality disorder.

The chapter on how all this affects you describes life in a pressure cooker. Family members start doubting their own memory, give up friends and hobbies, and get stuck in what the book calls FOG: fear, obligation and guilt. Elsewhere the authors sketch the stages family members tend to pass through: confusion before they have a name for the problem, a long stretch of trying to change the other person, a turn inward to look at their own part, a decision about the relationship, and finally living with that decision.

The second half is practical. It starts with changes inside yourself. The "three Cs" say you didn't cause the disorder, can't control it and can't cure it. The book pictures you as a lighthouse, which can shine a light but can't wade out and steer the ship. Don't take the attacks personally, rebuild a life of your own, and let go of the hope that the right words will make things normal.

Then come skills. Work out your limits before a crisis, and make each one something you control ("I will leave the room if you shout") rather than a rule the other person has to obey. Communicate so you can be heard: stay calm, don't argue over what "really" happened, acknowledge feelings without accepting blame, and use a structured method called DEAR (describe, express, assert, reinforce) to state what you need. Hold the line every time, because giving in now and then teaches that pushing works.

Later chapters deal with harder situations. There is a safety plan for rages, violence and threats of suicide, which are always taken seriously and handed to professionals. There is advice on shielding children, a chapter for parents whose own child has BPD, and one on "distortion campaigns", where the person spreads lies or false accusations, often during a divorce, and the advice is to stay calm, keep records and get legal help. The book ends by helping readers decide whether to stay, step back or leave. It gives weight to one question above the rest: is the person willing to get treatment?

## Key ideas

**You can only change yourself.** No argument, sacrifice or perfect sentence will make the person better. Your power lies in how you respond and what you are willing to accept.

**Splitting.** People with BPD tend to see others as all good or all bad, and switch between the two. Being idealised one week and hated the next is part of the disorder, not a fair judgement of you.

**Conventional and unconventional BPD.** Some people with BPD turn their pain inward and seek help. Many others seem to function well, deny any problem and aim their distress at family members.

**FOG.** Fear, obligation and guilt keep family members stuck and make it hard to see clearly. Naming them is the first step to making choices for yourself.

**The three Cs.** You didn't cause it, you can't control it and you can't cure it. The slogan is meant to stop readers blaming themselves for the other person's behaviour.

**Limits you enforce yourself.** A useful boundary describes what you will do, not what the other person must do. It only works if you keep it consistently, since occasional giving in rewards the behaviour.

**Be a mirror, not a sponge.** When blame or rage comes your way, don't soak it up. Calmly hand responsibility back to the person it belongs to.

**Safety comes first.** Rages, violence and suicide threats call for a plan made in advance, including when to leave and when to call for help.

## If you read one chapter

Chapter 5, "Making Changes Within Yourself," because it is where the book turns from explaining the other person to what the reader can actually do.
