## In short

Nedra Glover Tawwab is a licensed therapist in North Carolina who became widely known for short posts on Instagram about boundaries. This book expands them into a guide. Her central claim is that much of the anxiety, resentment and burnout her clients bring to therapy comes down to limits they never stated, and that stating them plainly usually saves relationships instead of ending them.

## Summary

Tawwab defines a boundary as an expectation or need that helps you feel safe and comfortable in a relationship. It is how you tell other people what you will and will not accept. She starts with what life looks like without boundaries. You say yes and mean no, you feel overwhelmed however much you get done, you resent the people who ask things of you, and you start avoiding them so they cannot ask. Burnout, anxiety and one-sided friendships follow. Each chapter opens with the story of a client in this position, and many end with reflection exercises and a short quiz.

She then asks why so many people struggle. Most of us learned what was allowed in the family we grew up in. If your parents never said no, or punished you when you did, stating a limit as an adult feels dangerous. Fear of seeming mean, of being disliked or of losing someone keeps people quiet. Tawwab is blunt that guilt and discomfort are normal when you start, and that they are not a sign you are doing something wrong.

A useful middle section describes three ways boundaries go. Porous boundaries let everything through: oversharing, people-pleasing, taking on other people's problems as your own. Rigid boundaries go too far the other way, with walls that keep everyone out and never bend. Healthy boundaries sit in between. They rest on knowing your own values and capacity, and they can flex with the situation.

She lists six types of boundaries. Physical ones cover your body and personal space. Sexual ones cover consent and preferences. Intellectual ones concern your thoughts and ideas and the right to hold them without being mocked. Emotional ones protect you from being dumped on or made responsible for someone else's feelings. Material ones cover money and possessions, such as whether you lend your car. Time boundaries cover how you spend your hours and energy. Violations can be small or large, and small ones repeated often do a lot of damage.

A chapter on trauma explains how growing up with neglect, abuse or addiction can push people toward enmeshment, where there is no clear line between you and a family member, or toward codependency, where your sense of worth depends on managing someone else. She also asks readers to look at the boundaries they keep with themselves, around spending, sleep, phone use and how they talk to themselves.

The practical method is simple. Be clear and direct, using plain statements like "I need" or "I'd like" without long explanations or apologies. Then act on it, because a limit you never enforce teaches people to ignore it. Expect pushback. People who benefited from your lack of boundaries may sulk, argue, test you or give you the silent treatment. Stay calm and consistent, and repeat yourself as often as needed.

Part Two applies all of this to specific areas. With family she covers parents who overstep, adult siblings, holidays and in-laws, and accepting that some relatives will not change, which leaves you deciding how much contact you can manage. With partners and friends the focus is on reciprocity and saying what you need early. At work she deals with overwork, bosses who text at night and colleagues who offload tasks. A final area is technology: social media, constant availability, and the limits you set on your own scrolling.

The book closes by reminding readers that setting boundaries is a practice. You will get it wrong sometimes, and each attempt makes the next one easier.

## Key ideas

**Clarity saves relationships.** Saying what you need early and plainly prevents the slow build-up of resentment that ends more relationships than any honest conversation does.

**Porous, rigid and healthy.** Boundaries can be too loose, too tight, or flexible and grounded in your own values. Most people swing between the first two.

**Six types.** Physical, sexual, intellectual, emotional, material and time. Thinking through each one shows where you are giving away more than you want to.

**Resentment is a signal.** Feeling resentful, overwhelmed or eager to avoid someone usually means a boundary is missing.

**State it, then act on it.** A boundary has two parts: the words and the follow-through. Without the second, people learn that your words do not count.

**Discomfort is expected.** Guilt and anxiety are normal when you begin, especially if your family discouraged limits. They fade with practice.

**You can only control your side.** Other people may not respect your limits. Your choice is how you respond, including seeing someone less.

## If you read one chapter

"The Six Types of Boundaries," because it gives you a map for spotting exactly where in your life the trouble lies.
