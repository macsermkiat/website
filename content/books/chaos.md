## In short

*Chaos: Making a New Science* tells how a scattered group of meteorologists, mathematicians, biologists and physicists found that tiny causes can have huge effects, and that behind apparently random behaviour there is often a hidden order. Gleick, then a science reporter for the New York Times, writes it as a story of people rather than equations. It was a finalist for both the Pulitzer Prize and the National Book Award.

## Summary

The book opens at Los Alamos in the 1970s with Mitchell Feigenbaum, a physicist who kept odd hours and took long night walks, puzzling over why nobody could explain something as ordinary as the swirl of a cloud or turbulence in a stream. Physics had mastered the very small and the very large, Gleick says, but it had looked away from the messy, everyday middle. Chaos was the science of that middle.

The first real chapter goes back to 1961 and Edward Lorenz, a meteorologist at MIT who ran a toy weather model of twelve equations on a Royal McBee computer. Wanting to rerun a forecast, he typed in a number from a printout, 0.506 instead of the full 0.506127. The new run started out matching the old one and then drifted until it bore no resemblance at all. That tiny rounding had changed the whole weather. This was sensitive dependence on initial conditions, later called the butterfly effect after a 1972 talk asking whether a butterfly in Brazil could set off a tornado in Texas. Lorenz boiled his model down to three equations for rising and falling air and found that their solutions traced a looping, never-repeating shape, now known as the Lorenz attractor.

"Revolution" describes how slowly these ideas spread. Gleick borrows Thomas Kuhn's picture of how science changes, and shows the mathematician Stephen Smale, who studied systems that stretch and fold like a baker's dough, arriving at similar ideas by a separate route. In "Life's Ups and Downs," the biologist Robert May studies a simple equation for animal populations that grow and then crash. Turn up the growth rate and the population stops settling down. It flips between two values, then four, then eight, and then goes wild. James Yorke and Tien-Yien Li proved a result about such equations in a 1975 paper and gave the field its name: chaos.

Benoit Mandelbrot takes over in "A Geometry of Nature." Working at IBM, he noticed that cotton prices showed the same kind of jagged pattern whether you looked at a day or a decade. He asked how long the coast of Britain is and pointed out that the answer depends on your ruler, because every bay has smaller bays within it. He called such self-similar shapes fractals, and he measured their roughness with dimensions that are not whole numbers.

"Strange Attractors" returns to turbulence. David Ruelle and Floris Takens proposed that the onset of turbulence might be governed by strange attractors like Lorenz's, and experimenters such as Harry Swinney and Jerry Gollub watched fluids between spinning cylinders to test it. Then comes the book's central discovery. In "Universality," Feigenbaum, working with a pocket calculator, found that the period-doubling route to chaos happens at the same rate in completely different equations. The gap before each new doubling shrinks by a factor of about 4.669. Wildly different systems share the same numbers.

That claim needed a real experiment. In "The Experimenter," Albert Libchaber, in Paris, built a tiny cell of liquid helium, heated it from below, and watched the convection go through the doubling cascade Feigenbaum had predicted.

The later chapters spread out. "Images of Chaos" covers the Mandelbrot set and the endlessly detailed boundaries mathematicians like John Hubbard found when they drew them with computers. "The Dynamical Systems Collective" follows a band of graduate students at Santa Cruz, including Robert Shaw, Doyne Farmer, Norman Packard and James Crutchfield, who studied a dripping tap and worked out how to find an attractor in a stream of real measurements. "Inner Rhythms" turns to the body: irregular heartbeats, fibrillation and the patterns in eye movements. The last chapter argues that chaos changed how scientists see the world, from a place of tidy order to one where simple rules produce endless complexity.

## Key ideas

**Sensitive dependence on initial conditions.** In a chaotic system, a tiny difference at the start grows until it swamps everything. This is why detailed weather forecasts fail after about a week or two.

**Deterministic but unpredictable.** Chaotic systems follow exact rules with no randomness built in. They are unpredictable because we can never know the starting state precisely enough.

**Strange attractors.** A chaotic system's behaviour never repeats, yet it stays confined to a particular shape in the space of possible states. The Lorenz attractor is the famous example.

**Period doubling.** As you push certain systems harder, their rhythm splits from one cycle to two, four, eight and so on, and then tips into chaos.

**Universality.** Feigenbaum found that the period-doubling cascade happens at the same rate, set by the number 4.669, in very different systems. The details of a system matter less than its overall shape.

**Fractals.** Many natural shapes, such as coastlines, clouds and blood vessels, look similarly rough at every scale. Mandelbrot gave them a geometry and a way to measure that roughness.

**Nonlinearity.** In nonlinear systems, effects are not proportional to causes, so small pushes can have big results. Most of nature works this way, even though classical physics mostly studied the linear cases.

## If you read one chapter

"The Butterfly Effect," because Lorenz's rounding error is the founding story of the whole field and Gleick tells it in vivid detail.
