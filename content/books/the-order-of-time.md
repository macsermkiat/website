## In short

Rovelli, a founder of loop quantum gravity, argues that the time we feel, one universal clock ticking forward for everyone, is a local and approximate thing. The first part strips away its features one by one, the second describes a world without time at all, and the third explains how our sense of time's flow grows out of entropy, blurred vision and memory. It came out in Italian in 2017 and in English in 2018, and it is short and written with a lot of literary colour.

## Summary

The title comes from Anaximander, the Greek philosopher who wrote that things change into one another "according to the order of time." Rovelli sets out to show that this order is less basic than it seems.

Part One, "The Crumbling of Time," starts with something easy to measure. A clock on a mountain runs slightly faster than a clock at sea level, because time slows near large masses. With good enough clocks you can see the difference over a height of a few centimetres. So there is no single time, only many local times. That is the "loss of unity."

Next, direction. Almost every fundamental law of physics works the same forward and backward. The exception is the one Rudolf Clausius spotted in the 1860s: heat flows from hot bodies to cold ones and never the reverse. Clausius named the quantity that grows in such processes entropy, and Ludwig Boltzmann later explained it by counting. There are far more disordered arrangements of molecules than ordered ones. Rovelli adds a twist: entropy depends on which details you ignore. The difference between past and future appears only when you look at the world in a blurred, approximate way.

Then the present goes. Einstein showed there is no common "now" across the universe. Asking what is happening right now on a planet like Proxima b, four light years away, has no answer; there is a whole span of times on Proxima b that are neither past nor future for us. Time also loses its independence. Aristotle thought of time as a measure of change, while Newton pictured an absolute time flowing on its own. Einstein combined the two: time is a field, part of the gravitational field, which moves and bends. Finally, quantum mechanics suggests time comes in tiny grains around the Planck time, and even the order of events can be in a quantum superposition.

Part Two, "The World Without Time," asks what is left. Rovelli's answer is that the world is made of events, not things. A kiss or a storm is plainly an event, a happening. A stone looks like a thing, but it too is a process that lasts a long time before it crumbles. He then argues that our language, built on tenses, misleads us, and he weighs the idea of a fixed "block universe" in which past and future are equally real, finding it no better than the idea that only the present exists. In loop quantum gravity, the equations contain no time variable. They describe how events relate to one another, not how things change against a background clock.

Part Three, "The Sources of Time," rebuilds our experience. With Alain Connes, Rovelli proposed that time emerges from our incomplete knowledge of the world: when you describe a system statistically, a flow of time appears. He calls this the idea that time is ignorance. The low entropy of the early universe may be low only from our particular point of view, the way the sky seems to turn around us because we stand on a spinning Earth.

From that starting point, the growth of entropy leaves traces: footprints, fossils, records and memories. We know the past and not the future because traces point only one way. Rovelli then turns to the human side, with the madeleine from Proust, whose taste brings back a whole childhood, and argues that each of us is held together by memory and anticipation, a story spread across time. The book ends on a personal note about mortality and why we can feel calm about a life that is limited in time.

## Key ideas

**Time is local.** Clocks run at different speeds depending on gravity and motion. There are many times, not one.

**The arrow comes from entropy.** The only basic law that tells past from future is that heat flows from hot to cold. Every other sign of time's direction traces back to growing entropy.

**No universal now.** Beyond our immediate surroundings, "now" has no meaning. For a distant planet there is a range of moments that are neither past nor future for us.

**Events, not things.** The world is better described as a network of happenings than as a collection of objects. Even a stone is a slow event.

**Fundamental physics has no time variable.** In quantum gravity, the equations describe how quantities relate to each other, not how they evolve against a clock.

**Time as blurring.** Our sense of time flowing appears when we describe the world without seeing its microscopic details. A perfect observer would not see the same flow.

**We are made of memory.** Traces of the past, stored in our brains, and our constant anticipation of what comes next, are what make time feel like it passes, and what hold our identity together.

## If you read one chapter

"Loss of Direction," because it shows how the whole difference between past and future hangs on heat and entropy, which the rest of the book keeps coming back to.
