## In short

This is less an autobiography than a pile of anecdotes, edited from taped conversations Feynman had with his friend and drumming partner Ralph Leighton. The stories run roughly in order through five parts of his life, and most of them are about him poking at something (a safe, the unwritten rules of a bar, a school textbook, a physics problem) until he understands how it works. Underneath the jokes is a consistent attitude: find out for yourself, don't be impressed by titles, and above all don't fool yourself.

## Summary

The first part, "From Far Rockaway to MIT", starts in the Depression. Young Feynman fixes neighbours' radios, and one man is amazed that the boy paces up and down thinking before he touches anything; the man goes around telling people this kid fixes radios by thinking. Working in a hotel kitchen he invents a faster way to cut string beans, which the cook ignores. At MIT he loves physics and dodges the humanities. The summer after he graduates, he is the only chemist at a small company trying to plate plastic with metal.

The second part covers graduate school at Princeton. The title comes from here. At a tea given by the dean's wife, asked whether he wants cream or lemon, he says both, and she replies with the book's title line. He volunteers to be hypnotised, asks for "a map of the cat" in a biology class because he does not know the names of the muscles, and gives his first seminar with Einstein, Wolfgang Pauli and John von Neumann in the audience. He learns he is good at integrals because he picked up an unusual trick, differentiating under the integral sign, from an old calculus book, so he has "a different box of tools" from everyone else. He also runs experiments on the ants in his room to work out how they find food.

The third part is the war. At Los Alamos he is young, junior and irreverent. He learns to open the combination locks on filing cabinets holding atomic secrets, partly to show how poor the security is, and he fights with the censors who read his letters. His wife Arline, ill with tuberculosis in a sanatorium in Albuquerque, sends him letters in code for fun. He is sent to Oak Ridge and realises the plant is storing uranium in ways that could set off a chain reaction, and has to explain the danger to the engineers. After the war the army's psychiatrist declares him unfit for service.

The fourth part runs from Cornell to Caltech, with a long stay in Brazil. After the war he feels burnt out and unable to do physics. He decides to stop worrying about importance and just play. Watching someone toss a plate in the Cornell cafeteria, he notices that the wobble and the spin of the Cornell emblem on it run at different rates, and sets out to work out the relation between them. That idle calculation, he says, led straight to the work on electrons that won him the Nobel Prize. In Brazil in the early 1950s he teaches physics and finds students who can recite definitions perfectly but cannot connect them to anything real, and he tells a room of officials that no science is being taught in their country. He also plays in a samba band at carnival and beats an abacus salesman at a cube root by knowing a shortcut. In Las Vegas and in bars he studies nightclub life like an experiment. He turns down a comfortable job at the Institute for Advanced Study, arguing that people with no teaching duties and no students run out of ideas.

The last part, "The World of One Physicist", is a mix. He describes the rare thrill of finding a new law of nature, in his work on the weak force. He serves on a California commission choosing school maths textbooks and finds other members rating a book whose inside pages were blank. He learns to draw from an artist friend and sells work under a pseudonym. He finds the Nobel Prize, which he shared in 1965, mostly a nuisance. He floats in sensory deprivation tanks to see what hallucinations are like.

The book ends with "Cargo Cult Science", his 1974 Caltech commencement speech. He compares shaky research to islanders who build runways and wooden control towers hoping planes will land. Everything looks right, but the essential thing is missing. For him that thing is a particular kind of honesty: reporting everything that could make your result wrong, and being hardest on your own ideas.

## Key ideas

**Knowing versus naming.** The Brazil chapters show students who can recite physics without understanding it. Feynman insists that knowledge means being able to use an idea, not repeat its words.

**Play leads to discovery.** His best work came after he gave up trying to do important physics and started playing with problems for fun, like the wobbling plate.

**A different box of tools.** Learning methods other people don't know lets you solve problems they can't. Feynman's odd integration trick made him look like a genius when he was simply equipped differently.

**Every lock has a weakness.** The safecracking stories show his habit of assuming any system built by people can be understood and beaten, and that it is fun to try.

**Titles don't impress nature.** Feynman is suspicious of pomp, committees, prestige and jargon. He judges people and ideas by whether they make sense, whoever they come from.

**Don't fool yourself.** The central warning of "Cargo Cult Science": the first person you must not fool is yourself, and you are the easiest to fool. Real science means going out of your way to report what might prove you wrong.

**Curiosity as a way of living.** Drumming, drawing, languages, safes, ants and hypnosis get the same attention as physics. The book presents curiosity as a whole temperament, applied everywhere.

## If you read one chapter

Read "Cargo Cult Science", the closing speech, because it says plainly the idea about honesty that all the funny stories are circling.
