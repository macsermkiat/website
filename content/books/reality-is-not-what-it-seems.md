## In short

First published in Italian in 2014 and in English in 2016, this is Rovelli's longer and more technical follow-up to his hit *Seven Brief Lessons on Physics*. It tells the history of physics as one long thread, from Democritus to Einstein to quantum mechanics, that leads to loop quantum gravity, the theory Rovelli helped create. Its claim is that space is made of finite grains, that time as we picture it does not exist at the deepest level, and that the world is a web of relations and information.

## Summary

Rovelli starts in ancient Greece, and his hero is Democritus. In the fifth century BCE, Leucippus and his student Democritus proposed that everything is made of tiny indivisible atoms moving through empty space. Rovelli admires this as the first attempt to explain the world without gods, and he follows its survival through Lucretius's long poem *On the Nature of Things*, lost for centuries and rediscovered in 1417. The key idea he takes from it is that nature is granular: you cannot divide things forever.

Part One continues with "The Classics." Newton explained why the Moon orbits and apples fall with one law of gravity, but left unexplained how that force acts across empty space. Faraday supplied a new picture, the field, a physical thing filling space and carrying forces along lines. Maxwell turned Faraday's lines into equations and showed that light is a ripple in the electromagnetic field.

Part Two covers the two revolutions of the twentieth century. In "Albert," Einstein shows that there is no universal "now": between your past and your future lies an extended present whose size depends on distance. Then comes general relativity, which Rovelli clearly regards as a masterpiece. Einstein saw that Newton's space and the gravitational field are one and the same thing. Space is not a stage; it is a field that bends, ripples and moves. Rovelli points out that Dante, in the *Paradiso*, described a universe with the shape of a three-dimensional sphere, one of the shapes Einstein's equations allow.

In "Quanta," Planck, Einstein, Bohr, Heisenberg and Dirac build quantum mechanics. Rovelli sums it up in three lessons. Things come in discrete amounts (granularity). The future is not fixed by the past (indeterminism). And the properties of an object exist only in its interactions with other objects (relationality). Heisenberg's insight, as Rovelli reads it, was to describe only how things act on one another, not what they are like between interactions.

Part Three is where the two revolutions collide. General relativity treats space as smooth and continuous; quantum mechanics says every field comes in quanta. So space itself should be quantum. Rovelli tells the story of Matvei Bronstein, a young Soviet physicist who first saw in the 1930s that this implies a smallest possible length, and who was executed in Stalin's purges in 1938. He then describes the Wheeler-DeWitt equation of the 1960s, a quantum equation for the universe with no time variable in it.

Next comes loop quantum gravity itself. Solving that equation leads to closed loops, like Faraday's field lines, and the loops link up into what are called spin networks. The nodes of the network are grains of space, each with a definite smallest volume, and the links show which grains touch. Space is not a container for these grains. The grains are the space. In "Time Does Not Exist," Rovelli extends this to time: clocks run at different speeds depending on gravity, and in the full theory the world is a network of events, a "spinfoam," with no single background time ticking along.

Part Four draws out consequences. If space cannot be squeezed infinitely small, the Big Bang may have been a Big Bounce from an earlier contracting universe. Black holes may not crush matter to a point but bounce back and eventually explode, perhaps visibly. Rovelli is honest that none of this has been confirmed yet, and he contrasts his approach with string theory, whose predicted supersymmetric particles did not appear at the Large Hadron Collider. The final chapters argue that quantum gravity removes infinity from physics, that information and correlation may be basic ingredients of the world, and that science is valuable because it lives with doubt rather than claiming certainty.

## Key ideas

**The world is granular.** From Democritus to quantum mechanics, the deepest discoveries have shown that nature cannot be divided forever. Rovelli extends this to space itself.

**Space is a field.** Einstein's general relativity showed that space is not an empty container but the gravitational field, something physical that bends and ripples.

**Three lessons of quantum mechanics.** Nature comes in discrete lumps, the future is open, and objects have properties only through their interactions with other objects.

**Loops and spin networks.** In loop quantum gravity, space is a network of tiny linked grains, each with a minimum size. The network does not sit in space; it is space.

**No universal time.** Time runs at different rates in different places, and in the fundamental theory there is no background clock at all, only events and their relations.

**The end of infinity.** A smallest length means no infinitely small points, so the singularities at the Big Bang and inside black holes may be replaced by bounces.

**Science as doubt.** Rovelli argues that science works because it keeps its answers open to revision. Admitting ignorance is where new understanding starts.

## If you read one chapter

"Quanta of Space," because it is the heart of the book, where Rovelli explains in plain terms how loops and spin networks turn space into grains.
