## In short

Scott Young is best known for working through the material of MIT's four-year computer science degree on his own in about a year. In this book he calls that kind of project "ultralearning": learning that is self-directed and intense. He tells the stories of other people who did something similar, then distils what they have in common into nine principles, from mapping a subject before you start to testing yourself instead of rereading.

## Summary

The book opens with Young's MIT Challenge. Using the lectures, assignments and exams that MIT posts free online, he worked through the 33 courses of its computer science curriculum in roughly twelve months, grading himself against the real exams. He followed it with a year of learning languages with his friend Vatsal "Vat" Jaiswal, spending about three months each in Spain, Brazil, China and South Korea and trying not to speak English at all.

He then introduces others who took on similar projects. Roger Craig prepared for the quiz show *Jeopardy!* by downloading thousands of past questions, sorting them by topic to see what came up most, and drilling himself with flashcard software; he went on to set what was then the record for the most money won in a single game. Eric Barone spent about five years teaching himself programming, pixel art, music and writing so he could build the farming game Stardew Valley alone. Young argues that the economy increasingly rewards rare, advanced skills like these, and that school often fails to teach them. Chapter 3 follows Tristan de Montebello, who went from almost no public speaking experience to the final of the Toastmasters world championship in seven months.

The rest of the book sets out nine principles, one per chapter, each with its own story.

The first, metalearning, means drawing a map before you set out. Ask why you're learning, what you need to learn (concepts, facts and procedures), and how others have learned it. Young suggests spending about a tenth of a project's time on this research.

Focus is illustrated by Mary Somerville, a Scottish woman of the early 1800s who taught herself mathematics against her family's wishes. Young treats it as three problems: getting started, staying on task, and matching your level of alertness to the job.

For directness, Young returns to Jaiswal, who couldn't find work in architecture until he learned the software and drawing skills firms wanted and built a portfolio to prove it. Skills learned in one setting transfer poorly to another, so practise as close as you can to real use.

Drill is Benjamin Franklin's chapter. He improved his writing by taking notes on essays he admired, rewriting them from his notes days later, and comparing the results with the originals. Find the weakest part of a skill, isolate it and practise it hard, then go back to the whole.

Retrieval starts with the Indian mathematician Srinivasa Ramanujan, who learned from a book that listed thousands of results with almost no proofs, so he had to work them out himself. Young cites research by Jeffrey Karpicke and Janell Blunt: students who read a text and then wrote down all they could recall outperformed students who made concept maps with the text in front of them. Rereading mostly makes you feel you know it.

Chris Rock, who tries out new jokes in small clubs and learns from the ones that fall flat, stands for feedback. Feedback can hurt as well as help, so seek specific, corrective feedback about the work, not praise or criticism of you.

Retention brings in Nigel Richards, a New Zealander who won the French-language Scrabble world championship without speaking French. Memories fade, so use spaced repetition, practise until skills become automatic, and overlearn the parts that matter most.

Intuition leans on Richard Feynman: don't give up on hard problems too soon, prove things to yourself, and explain an idea in plain words to find where your understanding breaks down.

Last comes experimentation, with Vincent van Gogh, who taught himself to draw through years of exercises and copying before finding his own style. Once you're past the basics, try different resources, techniques and constraints rather than only following others.

The last two chapters turn practical and then personal. Young gives a plan for a first project: research, schedule, execute, review, and then decide whether to maintain the skill, let it lapse or go deeper. He ends with László Polgár, who raised his three daughters as chess players; Judit Polgár became the strongest female player in history. Young takes it as a case that intense learning can be joyful rather than forced.

## Key ideas

**Ultralearning.** A self-directed, intense way of learning a hard skill quickly. You choose the goal and the method, and you push yourself further than a class would.

**Metalearning.** Before you begin, map what you need to learn, how others learned it, and which resources to use. A few days of research can save months.

**Directness.** Learn by doing the thing itself, or something very close to it. Skills learned in the abstract often fail to transfer to real use.

**Drill the weakest link.** Break a skill into parts, find the one holding you back, and practise it on its own before putting the skill back together.

**Retrieval beats review.** Recalling information from memory, through free recall or self-made questions, strengthens learning far more than rereading notes.

**Feedback about the work.** Seek specific, corrective feedback that tells you what to change. Feedback aimed at you as a person can backfire.

**Spacing and overlearning.** Spread practice over time and push key skills to the point of automatic use, so they last after the project ends.

**The Feynman technique.** Explain a concept in simple terms as if teaching a beginner. The spots where you stumble show you what you don't yet understand.

## If you read one chapter

The chapter on retrieval, "Test to Learn," because its advice to test yourself instead of rereading is the easiest change to make and does the most good.
