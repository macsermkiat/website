## In short

*Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion* is Robert Cialdini's account of why people agree to things. A psychology professor at Arizona State, he combined laboratory research with years spent undercover in sales, fundraising and advertising training programmes. He found that most persuasion rests on six principles (reciprocation, commitment and consistency, social proof, liking, authority and scarcity), each a useful shortcut that can be turned against us. A 2021 expanded edition added a seventh, unity.

## Summary

Cialdini opens by admitting he has always been an easy mark for salespeople and charity collectors, which is what drew him to the subject. His first chapter explains the mechanism. Many animals respond to a single trigger with a fixed pattern of behaviour. A mother turkey will care for anything that makes a chick's "cheep-cheep" sound, even a stuffed polecat, its natural enemy, fitted with a tape recorder. People have similar automatic responses. They save effort most of the time, and a skilled persuader can set them off on purpose. A jewellery shop owner in Arizona, trying to clear some slow-selling turquoise, accidentally doubled the price instead of halving it, and it sold out, because shoppers took the high price as a sign of quality. In Ellen Langer's photocopier study, people let someone cut in line far more often when the request included the word "because," even when the reason given was empty.

Reciprocation comes first among the principles. We feel obliged to repay what others give us, even if we never asked for it. The Hare Krishna movement raised large sums at airports by pressing a flower into travellers' hands before asking for a donation. A related tactic is rejection-then-retreat: make a large request, and when it is refused, retreat to the smaller one you wanted all along. A Boy Scout once sold Cialdini chocolate bars this way after he declined five-dollar circus tickets. The other person's concession feels like a gift that calls for one in return.

Commitment and consistency is the second. Once we take a stand, we feel pressure to act in line with it. Bettors at a racetrack grow more confident in their horse right after placing the bet. In a 1966 study, homeowners who first agreed to put a tiny "drive safely" sign in their window were far more willing, weeks later, to accept a huge, ugly billboard on their lawn. Cialdini shows how Chinese captors in the Korean War got American prisoners to write mildly pro-communist statements that slowly shifted their views, and how car dealers "lowball" customers with a price that disappears once they have committed. Commitments stick best when they are active, public, effortful and freely chosen.

Social proof is the third. When unsure, we look at what people like us are doing. This explains canned laughter on television and bartenders seeding tip jars. In the 1964 murder of Kitty Genovese in New York, reports said many neighbours heard her cries and none helped. Cialdini uses research by Latané and Darley to argue that bystanders in a crowd each read the others' inaction as a sign that nothing is wrong. His grimmest example is the mass deaths at Jonestown in 1978.

Liking is the fourth. We say yes to people we like, and we like people who are attractive, similar to us, complimentary and associated with good things. Tupperware parties work because the request comes from a friend. The car salesman Joe Girard sent thousands of customers a card every month saying simply that he liked them.

Authority is the fifth. Stanley Milgram's experiments showed ordinary people giving what they believed were dangerous electric shocks because a man in a lab coat told them to. Titles, uniforms and expensive cars all trigger the same deference, and in one study most nurses were ready to give a patient an unsafe dose of a drug on the phoned instruction of an unknown "doctor".

Scarcity is the sixth. Things seem more valuable when they are rare or disappearing. In one study people rated cookies from a jar holding two as tastier than identical cookies from a jar holding ten, especially when the jar had just been emptied. Limited editions, deadlines and banned books all draw on this.

Each chapter ends with a section on defence. Cialdini does not ask readers to distrust every shortcut, since they usually serve us well. He asks them to notice when one is being faked, and to judge the offer on its merits.

## Key ideas

**Click, whirr.** People often respond automatically to a single cue, like a recording that starts playing. These shortcuts are efficient but easy to exploit.

**Reciprocation.** We feel obliged to return favours, gifts and concessions, even unwanted ones. A small gift before a request raises compliance sharply.

**Commitment and consistency.** Once we take a position, especially a public, written or effortful one, we tend to act in line with it. Small first steps lead to larger ones.

**Social proof.** In uncertain situations we copy people similar to us. It works most strongly when the crowd is large and like us.

**Liking.** We agree more readily with people we find attractive, similar, complimentary or connected to things we like.

**Authority.** We defer to experts and to symbols of authority such as titles and uniforms, sometimes against our own judgement.

**Scarcity.** Opportunities seem more valuable as they become less available, and losing a freedom makes us want it more.

**Unity.** Added in the 2021 edition: we are more easily influenced by people we see as part of our own group, such as family, hometown or team.

## If you read one chapter

"Commitment and Consistency," because it explains how small agreements quietly lead people to much bigger ones, including the reader.
