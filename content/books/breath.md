## In short

James Nestor, a science journalist, argues that most of us breathe poorly: through the mouth, too fast and too much. He tests this on himself, spending ten days with his nose plugged at Stanford, then goes looking for the scientists, doctors and self-taught "pulmonauts" who have studied breathing. His conclusion is that breathing slowly, lightly and through the nose can help with problems from snoring and anxiety to high blood pressure, and that anyone can learn it.

## Summary

Nestor came to the subject with weak lungs and repeated bouts of pneumonia. A doctor suggested a breathing class, so he went to a Sudarshan Kriya session in an old Victorian house in San Francisco. An hour in, he was drenched in sweat in a cool room, and the next day he felt calmer than he had in a long time. The book is his decade-long attempt to understand what happened.

It opens with an experiment. At Stanford, the rhinologist Jayakar Nayak ran a study on Nestor and Anders Olsson, a Swedish breathing teacher. For ten days they wore silicone plugs in their noses and breathed only through their mouths. Then, for ten days, they breathed only through their noses. The mouth-breathing phase was grim. Nestor's blood pressure and stress markers rose, his snoring soared, and within days he had sleep apnea. When they switched back to nose breathing, the snoring and apnea largely disappeared and his blood pressure fell.

Next he asks why humans breathe so badly in the first place. Our skulls give part of the answer. Early humans had wide jaws, broad mouths and straight teeth. As we began to eat softer food, first through cooking and much later through farming and industrial processing, we chewed less, and our faces and mouths grew smaller. The result is crooked teeth, narrow airways and a species unusually prone to blocked noses. He cites figures suggesting that about 40 per cent of people have chronic nasal obstruction and roughly half are habitual mouth breathers.

The middle part of the book goes through the "lost art" one habit at a time.

The first is the nose. It filters, warms and moistens air, and nasal breathing releases nitric oxide, which widens blood vessels. Nestor draws on the painter George Catlin, who travelled among Native American tribes in the 1830s and was struck by how carefully they taught children to breathe through their noses. The practical upshot is simple: keep your mouth closed, including at night. Nestor tapes his mouth shut when he sleeps.

The second is the exhale. Large studies have linked lung capacity with how long people live, and lungs can be trained. He tells the story of Katharina Schroth, a German teenager who treated her own scoliosis with breathing exercises in the early 1900s, and Carl Stough, a choral conductor who taught emphysema patients, and later the US track team for the 1968 Olympics, to breathe out fully.

The third is breathing slowly. Here Nestor explains why carbon dioxide matters: without enough of it in the blood, oxygen does not leave the blood for the tissues as easily. He notes that many prayers and chants, from the rosary to Buddhist mantras, slow the breath to about six breaths a minute. His recommended rhythm is a 5.5-second inhale and a 5.5-second exhale.

The fourth is breathing less. The Ukrainian doctor Konstantin Buteyko found that many of his patients with asthma and heart disease were chronically overbreathing, and he treated them by teaching them to breathe less. Nestor also cites a more recent trial in which asthma patients who learned to breathe less, and so raise their carbon dioxide, saw their symptoms drop sharply.

The fifth is chewing. Comparing ancient and modern skulls, he shows how shrinking jaws narrow the airway, a problem the dentist Weston Price had tied to modern diets back in the 1930s. He tries a palate-expanding device and hard chewing, and after a year scans show new bone in his face.

The last part, "Breathing+," covers techniques that deliberately push the body. He describes Tummo, the Tibetan "inner fire" practice, and Wim Hof, the Dutchman who used a version of it to withstand long spells in ice and, in a 2011 study, to resist the effects of an injected bacterial toxin. He looks at breath-holding, and at research suggesting that some anxiety disorders involve an oversensitivity to carbon dioxide, which breath training may calm.

The epilogue boils it down. Breathe through your nose. Breathe slowly and a little less than you think you need. Breathe out fully. Chew hard food. Once in a while, try breathing hard or holding your breath on purpose.

## Key ideas

**Breathe through your nose.** The nose cleans and warms air and adds nitric oxide. Mouth breathing, especially at night, is linked to snoring, sleep apnea and poorer sleep.

**We evolved into bad breathers.** Softer diets shrank our jaws and mouths over thousands of years, leaving narrower airways and more blocked noses.

**Carbon dioxide is not waste.** You need enough of it in the blood for oxygen to reach the tissues. Overbreathing blows it off and can leave the body short of oxygen where it matters.

**Slow down.** A pace of about 5.5 seconds in and 5.5 seconds out, around six breaths a minute, calms the nervous system and appears in prayer and meditation traditions worldwide.

**Breathe less.** Buteyko's method treats asthma and other conditions by training people to take in less air, and Nestor thinks most of us overbreathe.

**Chew.** Hard, chewy food helps jaws develop and keeps airways open, and even adults can gain some bone.

**Sometimes breathe hard.** Tummo, the Wim Hof method and breath-holding are short, deliberate stresses that may train the body to handle stress better.

## If you read one chapter

"Slow," because it explains the carbon dioxide idea that the rest of the book depends on and gives the one breathing pattern you can start using straight away.
