## In short

Walter Isaacson follows Einstein from a curious boy in Germany to an unemployed graduate, a patent clerk who remade physics in one year, the most famous scientist in the world, and finally a lonely holdout against quantum mechanics in Princeton. The book's argument is that his distrust of authority, the same trait that made him a difficult student and a free-thinking citizen, is what let him see what others missed. Isaacson explains relativity and the light quantum in plain language, and uses Einstein's personal letters to show a warm, funny and often selfish man.

## Summary

Einstein was born in Ulm in 1879 and grew up in Munich. Isaacson picks out a few moments from childhood that Einstein himself remembered: the compass his father showed him when he was about five, whose needle was pushed by something invisible, and a geometry book that delighted him as a boy. He hated the drill and rote learning of his German school and left it as a teenager. At a more relaxed school in Aarau, Switzerland, he began imagining what it would be like to ride alongside a beam of light, a thought experiment he kept returning to for years.

At the Zurich Polytechnic he skipped lectures he found dull, annoyed his professors, and fell in love with a fellow physics student, Mileva Marić. After graduating in 1900 he could not get an academic job. The letters show the strain: a daughter, Lieserl, born to Mileva before they married, whose fate is unknown, and parents who opposed the match. In 1902 a friend's father helped him get a post as a technical examiner at the patent office in Bern.

Isaacson argues the patent office was good for him. It paid, left him time to think, and trained him to look hard at the physics behind inventions, including devices for synchronising clocks across distances. In 1905, at twenty-six, he published a series of papers that changed physics. One proposed that light comes in packets, or quanta. One gave a way to measure the size of molecules, and another explained the jittering of particles in water as the result of molecules bumping into them. The paper on special relativity threw out absolute time: two observers moving relative to each other will disagree about whether events are simultaneous. A short follow-up showed that mass and energy are equivalent, the idea behind E=mc².

Academic jobs followed within a few years. He moved through professorships in Zurich and Prague and in 1914 took a post in Berlin, where his marriage fell apart. Meanwhile he worked on extending relativity to gravity. His "happiest thought", in 1907, was that a person falling freely would not feel their own weight, so gravity and acceleration might be the same thing. Turning that into a theory took eight years and a new kind of mathematics, which his old classmate Marcel Grossmann helped him learn. In November 1915, racing against the mathematician David Hilbert, he presented the finished equations of general relativity. They correctly accounted for a small wobble in the orbit of Mercury.

In 1919 a British eclipse expedition led by Arthur Eddington measured starlight bending around the sun by the amount he had predicted, and newspapers made him a celebrity overnight. He divorced Mileva, promising her the money from a Nobel Prize he had not yet won, and married his cousin Elsa. The prize came, for 1921, for his work on the light quantum rather than relativity.

The second half of the book is about a rebel growing stubborn. Einstein helped start quantum theory but could not accept where it went, with its randomness and uncertainty. In long debates with Niels Bohr he tried to find holes in it, and his 1935 paper with Podolsky and Rosen pointed to the strange linked behaviour now called entanglement. Most physicists moved on without him. He spent his last three decades searching for a unified field theory that he never found.

His public life grew just as large. He was a pacifist in the First World War and an outspoken Jew in Weimar Germany, and he left for the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton when Hitler took power in 1933. In 1939 he signed the letter to Roosevelt about a possible atomic bomb, which he later regretted. He supported civil rights, spoke out against McCarthyism, and turned down the presidency of Israel. He died in 1955, still working on his equations.

## Key ideas

**Curiosity and imagination.** Isaacson presents Einstein's genius as a matter of wonder more than raw mathematical power. His breakthroughs often began as pictures in his head, like chasing a light beam or a man falling off a roof.

**Thought experiments.** Einstein reasoned by imagining simple physical situations and following them strictly to their conclusions. This let him see the flaws in accepted ideas before he had the maths to fix them.

**Relativity of time.** There is no single clock for the universe. Observers moving relative to each other measure time and distance differently, while the speed of light stays the same for all of them.

**Gravity is geometry.** In general relativity, mass curves space and time, and objects move along the curves. That is why starlight bends near the sun.

**Nonconformity as a method.** Isaacson's central claim is that Einstein's refusal to accept authority, in school, in physics and in politics, was the source of his originality. Freedom of thought mattered to him in science and in society alike.

**The rebel becomes the holdout.** The same independence that let him overturn Newton made him refuse to accept quantum randomness. His phrase that God does not play dice sums up that resistance.

**A flawed private man.** The newly available letters show Einstein could be cold to his wives and his sons even while he was warm and witty in public. Isaacson does not hide this.

## If you read one chapter

Read the chapter on 1905, the "miracle year", where Isaacson shows how a patent clerk with no university post wrote the papers that changed physics.
