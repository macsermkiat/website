## In short

Dale Carnegie had taught evening classes in public speaking and human relations in New York since 1912 when a publisher at Simon & Schuster, Leon Shimkin, sat in on his course and persuaded him to turn it into a book. Published in 1936, it has sold more than thirty million copies. Its message is simple and old-fashioned in the best sense: people respond to being appreciated, listened to and understood, and they resist being criticised, corrected or ordered about.

## Summary

The book is a series of short chapters, each built around one principle and a string of anecdotes about presidents, businessmen, and Carnegie's own students. The modern edition, revised in 1981, groups them into four parts.

Part One sets out three fundamentals for handling people. The first is don't criticise, condemn or complain. Carnegie points out that almost nobody blames themselves for anything. The gunman "Two Gun" Crowley, captured after a shootout in New York in 1931, described himself as having a kind heart, and Al Capone saw himself as a public benefactor. If criminals feel this way, he argues, criticism of ordinary people only makes them defensive. He mentions a sharp letter Abraham Lincoln wrote to General Meade after Gettysburg and never sent. The second principle is to give honest, sincere appreciation, because the desire to feel important is one of the deepest human wants. Charles Schwab, whom Andrew Carnegie paid a famous salary to run his steel business, credited his success to praising people generously. Carnegie separates this from flattery, which is insincere and easy to spot. The third principle is to arouse in the other person an eager want. He uses fishing as an image: you may love strawberries and cream, but you bait the hook with worms, because that is what the fish want. Talk about what the other person wants.

Part Two offers six ways to make people like you. Become genuinely interested in other people. Smile. Remember that a person's name is, to them, the sweetest sound in any language; Andrew Carnegie named businesses after people he wanted to win over, and the politician Jim Farley was said to know fifty thousand people by name. Be a good listener and encourage others to talk about themselves. Carnegie tells of a dinner party where he spent the evening listening to a botanist, barely speaking, and was later praised as a fine conversationalist. Talk in terms of the other person's interests, as Theodore Roosevelt did when he read up on a visitor's subject the night before. And make the other person feel important, and do it sincerely.

Part Three, the longest, has twelve ways to win people to your way of thinking. The first is that the only way to win an argument is to avoid it, since even if you win, the other person resents you. Show respect for others' opinions and never tell someone flatly that they are wrong. If you are wrong, admit it quickly and emphatically. Begin in a friendly way. Get the other person saying "yes" early, a method Carnegie credits to Socrates. Let them do most of the talking, and let them feel the idea is theirs. Try honestly to see things from their point of view, and be sympathetic to their ideas and desires. Appeal to nobler motives. Dramatise your ideas, and when all else fails, throw down a challenge. Schwab once chalked the number of heats the day shift had produced on a mill floor, and the night shift worked to beat it.

Part Four is about leading people without causing resentment. Begin with praise and honest appreciation. Point out mistakes indirectly, and talk about your own mistakes before criticising someone else's. Ask questions instead of giving direct orders. Let the other person save face. Praise every improvement, however small. Give people a fine reputation to live up to. Use encouragement, and make faults seem easy to correct. Make the other person happy about doing what you suggest.

The original 1936 edition also had sections on writing effective business letters and on keeping a happy home life. These were dropped in the 1981 revision, which also updated many of the examples.

Carnegie is open that none of this is new. His point is that most people know these rules and do not follow them, and he asks readers to reread the book regularly and practise the principles on real people.

## Key ideas

**Don't criticise, condemn or complain.** Criticism puts people on the defensive and rarely changes them. Nearly everyone believes they are in the right.

**Honest, sincere appreciation.** People crave feeling important. Real, specific praise is powerful; flattery is hollow and people see through it.

**Start from what they want.** To move someone, talk about their interests, not yours. Bait the hook with what the fish likes.

**Be genuinely interested.** You make more friends in two months by being interested in others than in two years by trying to interest them in you.

**Names and listening.** Remembering someone's name and letting them talk about themselves are among the simplest ways to make them feel valued.

**Avoid arguments.** Winning an argument usually loses the person. Respect their view, admit your own errors quickly, and find points of agreement first.

**Let them save face.** When correcting someone, praise first, point out mistakes indirectly and give them a good reputation to live up to.

## If you read one chapter

"If You Want to Gather Honey, Don't Kick Over the Beehive," the opening chapter on criticism, because it makes the case for everything else in the book.
