## In short

Will Guidara ran the dining room at Eleven Madison Park in New York from 2006 until 2019, and in that time the restaurant went from a pleasant brasserie to number one on the World's 50 Best list. The book is part memoir and part management guide. Its argument is that service is getting things right, hospitality is how you make people feel, and that a business can win by being generous past the point that seems sensible.

## Summary

Guidara grew up in the restaurant trade. His father, Frank, was a senior executive at big restaurant companies, and for his twelfth birthday he took Will to dinner at the Four Seasons in Manhattan. Being looked after that well, as a boy, decided his career on the spot. His mother had been ill for most of his childhood and died soon after he left college, and a few weeks later the chef Daniel Boulud gave him a table, a meal and no bill. He remembers it as the moment he understood what hospitality can do for someone at a bad time.

After the hotel school at Cornell, he took his father's advice and learned the business side first. At Restaurant Associates he worked as an assistant purchaser and then at Nick + Stef's steakhouse in Madison Square Garden, where he learned to watch costs. He then joined Danny Meyer's Union Square Hospitality Group to run the cafés at the Museum of Modern Art. Meyer's idea of "enlightened hospitality", that you look after your staff first so they can look after guests, became Guidara's foundation. At MoMA he also worked out what he later called the 95/5 rule: manage almost everything to the penny, and then spend the last bit freely on the touches guests will remember.

In 2006 Meyer made him general manager of Eleven Madison Park, alongside a young Swiss chef, Daniel Humm. The early chapters are about the friction between kitchen and dining room and how the two men learned to work as partners. They set out to win four stars from the New York Times. After a three-star review in 2007, the team spent about two years serving every table as if the critic, Frank Bruni, were sitting at it, and in 2009 they got the fourth star.

The turning point came in 2010. Eleven Madison Park made the World's 50 Best list at number 50, the last spot. In the taxi afterwards Guidara and Humm wrote on a napkin that they would be number one. The food was already excellent, so Guidara decided the way up was hospitality so personal that it seemed out of proportion. That is where the book's title comes from.

The hot dog story is the core example. A table of tourists at lunch mentioned that they had eaten at all the city's famous restaurants but never had a New York street hot dog. Guidara ran outside, bought one from a cart for a couple of dollars, and got the kitchen to cut and plate it as a course. The guests talked about it more than anything else in the meal. His lesson was that the most memorable gestures come from paying attention, not from spending money.

To make this repeatable, the restaurant created a full-time job for it, the Dreamweaver, and the staff started calling these gestures Legends. For a family whose children had never seen snow, the team bought sleds and had a car waiting to take them to Central Park after dinner. Guests celebrating an engagement might go home with champagne flutes. Anyone on staff who overheard a detail could pass it on.

The later chapters widen out to management. Guidara writes about hiring for kindness, about sweating the smallest details, about letting staff own problems, and about tools like a signal staff could use when they were overwhelmed. In 2011 he and Humm bought the restaurant from Meyer, and they later opened the NoMad. They relaunched Eleven Madison Park with a more playful, New York-themed menu, and in 2017 it was named the best restaurant in the world.

The partnership ended in 2019, when Humm bought him out. Guidara closes by arguing that the ideas travel well beyond restaurants: any shop, office or team can decide to give people more than they expect.

## Key ideas

**Service versus hospitality.** Service is the technical part: the right dish at the right time. Hospitality is how the guest feels while it happens, and it is what people remember.

**The 95/5 rule.** Run 95 percent of the business with strict discipline over costs. Spend the remaining 5 percent generously, even extravagantly, on the moments that make an impression.

**Legends and the hot dog.** A small gesture built on something a guest let slip can mean more than an expensive one. The trick is to listen for it.

**The one-inch rule.** Stay careful all the way to the end. A plate should be set down gently in the last inch, and the same goes for the last step of any task.

**Look after the team first.** Borrowed from Danny Meyer: staff who feel respected and trusted will pass that feeling on to guests. Guidara treats culture as something a leader has to build on purpose.

**Hire for kindness.** Skills can be taught, but the instinct to notice and help people mostly cannot, so that is what to look for when hiring.

**Aim absurdly high.** The napkin promise to go from 50th to first gave the whole team a clear, shared goal, and it took seven years to reach.

## If you read one chapter

Read the one with the hot dog and the birth of the Dreamweaver role, because it holds the whole philosophy in a single story.
