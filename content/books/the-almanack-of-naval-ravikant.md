## In short

Naval Ravikant co-founded Epinions and AngelList and was an early investor in Uber and Twitter, but he is better known online for his short, blunt ideas about money and the mind. Eric Jorgenson, a product strategist, gathered those ideas from a decade of tweets, blog posts and podcast interviews and stitched them into one text in Naval's own words. The first half argues that getting rich is a skill anyone can learn; the second, that happiness is a skill too.

## Summary

Jorgenson did not write this book so much as edit it. He warns at the start that everything is taken out of context and that readers should "interpret generously." Tim Ferriss, who says he never writes forewords, wrote one anyway, partly because the book is given away free as an ebook and Naval earns nothing from it.

It opens with Naval's life. Born in Delhi in 1974, he moved to Queens at nine with his mother and brother. The family was poor, his parents had split up, and the public library became his after-school home. Passing the entrance exam for Stuyvesant, New York's selective high school, led to Dartmouth, where he studied computer science and economics, and then to Silicon Valley.

Part One, on wealth, is built around his widely shared 2018 tweetstorm, "How to Get Rich (without getting lucky)," which the book prints in full and then unpacks. The first move is to separate three things people confuse. Wealth, in his words, is "having assets that earn while you sleep." Money is how we move time and wealth around. Status is your rank in a social pecking order, and status games are zero-sum, while creating wealth is not. You will not get rich renting out your hours, Naval says. You need to own a piece of something.

His formula has three parts. Specific knowledge is the thing you know that cannot be taught in a classroom, usually found by following your own curiosity; he says it "feels like play to you and looks like work to others." Accountability means taking risks under your own name, so that you get the credit when it goes well and the blame when it does not. Leverage multiplies what you do. The old kinds are labour and capital, and both need someone's permission. The new kinds, code and media, need no one's permission, and they are the source of most new fortunes. If you cannot code, he says, write, record podcasts or make videos. His shorthand for all this is "productize yourself."

The rest of the wealth section is practical advice. Play long-term games with long-term people, because returns in money, relationships and knowledge all compound. Set a high imaginary hourly rate (his, before he had money, was $5,000) and refuse to spend time on tasks worth less. Put real thought into the three biggest decisions of early life: where you live, who you are with, and what you do. He also lists four kinds of luck, from blind chance, to luck you stir up by hustling, to luck you spot because you know a field well, to luck that comes looking for you because of your reputation.

The wealth part ends with judgment, which Naval rates above hard work. Clear thinking means dropping identities that lock you into positions, using mental models from evolution, economics and mathematics, and reading a lot. His advice on reading: "read what you love until you love to read."

Part Two, on happiness, begins with his claim that he went from about 3 out of 10 to 9 out of 10 and that money was a small part of it. He treats happiness as a default state, what is left when you stop feeling that something is missing. Desire, in his best-known line, is "a contract that you make with yourself to be unhappy until you get what you want," so he tries to hold one big desire at a time. Envy fades once you notice you would not swap your whole life, every part of it, with the person you envy.

The practical chapters cover habits: daily exercise, less alcohol and caffeine, more sunlight, and an hour of meditation each morning, which he calls intermittent fasting for the mind. In any situation you can change it, accept it or leave it. The book closes with his "rational Buddhism," keeping the practices he can verify and dropping the rest, and with the view that life has no meaning handed to it from outside, so you make your own, and that the present moment is all anyone has. A bonus section lists the books he recommends.

## Key ideas

**Wealth, money and status are different.** Wealth is, in Naval's words, "having assets that earn while you sleep," money is how it moves, and status is rank. Chase wealth, because it is positive-sum.

**Specific knowledge.** The skills that make you rich cannot be trained into you. They come from genuine curiosity, and Naval says they feel "like play to you" while "looking like work to others."

**Accountability.** Put your name on what you do. Owning the risk is how you earn the reward and the trust.

**Permissionless leverage.** Labour and capital need someone to give them to you. Code and media do not, and they keep working after you stop.

**Compound interest everywhere.** Money, reputation, knowledge and relationships all grow fastest when you stick with the same people and projects for years.

**Desire is chosen unhappiness.** Each thing you want is a small promise to be unhappy until you have it, so choose desires carefully and keep few.

**Happiness is a skill.** Peace of mind can be trained through habits like meditation, exercise and acceptance, the same way you train a body.

## If you read one chapter

"Understand How Wealth Is Created," because it holds the full "How to Get Rich" tweetstorm, and nearly everything else in the book expands on those few pages.
