## In short

*Captivate: The Science of Succeeding with People* treats social skill as something you can study and practise. Vanessa Van Edwards, who calls herself a recovering awkward person, ran experiments with her Science of People lab and pulled in academic research to build fourteen practical techniques. The book follows a relationship from the first handshake to the first few days of real contact.

## Summary

Van Edwards starts from her own discomfort at parties and networking events. She had tried to fix it with charm and chatter, and it had not worked. What changed things was treating people as puzzles to be curious about. During one month she even kept quiet at professional events and communicated with flash cards, and she found that people opened up more when she mostly listened.

Part One is the first five minutes. The first chapter says you do not have to be good at every kind of social event. Work out where you shine and where you struggle, and spend your energy there. At a party, she maps the room into zones. The entrance is a poor place to meet anyone because arrivals are busy getting their bearings. Hovering near the food or the bathrooms is a trap. The best spot is where people have a drink in hand and are ready to talk, such as just past the bar.

Next comes the "triple threat" of first impressions: keep your hands visible, stand tall in what she calls the launch stance, and make steady eye contact. Then she asks you to swap stock openers for "conversation sparks". Instead of "How's work?" try asking about an exciting project; instead of "How are you?" ask about the best part of their day. Questions like these give people a small lift of pleasure, and that makes the conversation more memorable.

Two more skills close the section. The "highlighter" is about drawing attention to other people's strengths, since people tend to rise to the labels they are given. "Thread theory" is her name for hunting for things in common through shared acquaintances, the situation you are both in, or shared interests. She also offers tricks for remembering names, such as repeating the name, picturing it spelled out and linking it to someone you already know.

Part Two covers the first five hours, when you move from impressions to understanding. She teaches the seven universal microexpressions (anger, contempt, happiness, fear, surprise, disgust and sadness), flashes that last under a second and can show what someone feels before they say it. She then uses the Big Five personality traits (openness, conscientiousness, extroversion, agreeableness and neuroticism) as a quick way to guess how someone likes to be approached. A planner high in conscientiousness wants the details; a person high in openness wants the new idea.

She adapts Gary Chapman's love languages for work, arguing that colleagues differ in how they like to be thanked: praise, gifts, help, time together or a pat on the back. Then she introduces six primary values that people chase: love, service, status, money, goods and information. You can often spot someone's main value by what they complain or boast about.

Part Three, the first five days, is about deepening and leading. The "story stack" means having a few good stories ready for common topics, told briefly with a hook and a struggle, then turning the conversation back with a question. To motivate people she recommends giving them ownership: explain why the goal matters, ask who has the relevant skill instead of assigning tasks at random, and let people choose how to do their part. She discusses the Benjamin Franklin effect, where asking someone for help or advice makes them like you more, as a reason to be willing to show some vulnerability.

For difficult people she gives a method she calls NUT: name the emotion you see, understand the fear underneath it, and transform the conversation by responding to that fear. She argues that most difficult behaviour comes from a handful of fears, such as being rejected, forgotten, criticised or out of control. The closing chapters encourage readers to keep practising and to put their energy into the people they most want in their lives.

## Key ideas

**Play to your strengths.** You do not have to enjoy every social setting. Choose the events and roles where you are at your best, and prepare for the rest.

**The triple threat.** Visible hands, confident posture and good eye contact shape a first impression before you say anything.

**Conversation sparks.** Swap routine questions for ones that invite a story or a feeling. People remember conversations that gave them a small lift.

**Thread theory.** Look for connections through people you both know, your shared situation or common interests. Each "me too" builds rapport.

**Speed-reading personality.** The Big Five traits give a quick sketch of how someone likes to communicate, so you can adapt your approach.

**Primary values.** People are driven mainly by one of six things: love, service, status, money, goods or information. Their complaints and brags give it away.

**Ask for help.** Asking for advice or a small favour draws people closer, because they get to show what they know.

## If you read one chapter

The chapter on conversation sparks, because the difference between a forgettable chat and a real one often comes down to a single better question.
