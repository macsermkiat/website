## In short

*Unfuck Your Boundaries: Build Better Relationships Through Consent, Communication, and Expressing Your Needs* is part of Faith G. Harper's series of small, plain-spoken self-help books from the independent publisher Microcosm. Harper is a licensed counsellor and certified sexologist with a practice in San Antonio, Texas. She treats boundaries as the basic way we tell other people what we need, and she puts consent, the body and social power at the centre of the discussion.

## Summary

Harper writes the way she talks to clients: casually, with plenty of swearing, jokes and asides, and with the science kept short. The book has four main parts, and it is brief enough to read in an evening.

The first part asks what boundaries are. Harper's definition is spatial: a boundary is where your space ends and someone else's begins. That space covers more than your body. She sorts boundaries into seven kinds. Physical boundaries concern touch and personal space. Property boundaries concern your belongings and money. Sexual boundaries concern intimacy and consent. Emotional or relational boundaries concern your feelings and how much personal information you share. Intellectual boundaries protect your right to your own thoughts and beliefs without being mocked. Spiritual boundaries cover faith and religious practice. Time boundaries cover how your hours and energy get spent.

Boundaries can also be too rigid, shutting out everyone, or too permeable, letting anyone walk over you. The aim is flexible boundaries that are firm where they need to be and open where it is safe. Consent runs through all of this. Harper treats it as something that applies well beyond sex: whether someone is happy to be hugged, to lend you something, or to talk about a painful subject.

The second part, on the brain and body, grounds the idea physically. Our nervous systems register a crossed boundary before our thinking mind does, as tension, unease or the urge to pull away. Harper encourages readers to notice those body signals, since many people have been trained to override them.

The third part asks how boundaries get damaged, and here the book differs most from other boundary guides. Harper looks at childhood and attachment: children who were not allowed to say no, or whose needs were ignored, grow into adults who either cannot set limits or set walls everywhere. Trauma pushes people toward the same extremes. She then widens the view to society. Social norms, hierarchies at work and in families, and cultural expectations all teach some people that their limits do not count. She points out that women, people of colour, queer people and disabled people face boundary violations routinely and are often punished for objecting. She also describes control and abuse, where someone steadily erodes another person's boundaries to dominate them, so readers can recognise it.

The final part is practical. Start by finding out what your boundaries are, through journaling, reflection and mindful attention to what makes you comfortable or uneasy. Then communicate them clearly, with "I" statements that describe your needs instead of attacking the other person. Harper is just as interested in the other side: hearing someone else's no without sulking or taking it as rejection, and remembering that a refusal is usually about the request, not about you. She also covers accountability. When you cross someone's boundary, name what you did, take responsibility without excuses, and change your behaviour.

She closes by arguing that personal boundaries and wider consent culture are connected. Respecting limits, one relationship at a time, is part of how people build safer communities. The book ends with resources for further reading.

## Key ideas

**A boundary marks your space.** It is the line where you end and another person begins, covering your body, belongings, feelings, ideas, beliefs and time.

**Seven kinds of boundary.** Physical, property, sexual, emotional, intellectual, spiritual and time. Checking each one shows where you are being pushed.

**Rigid, permeable or flexible.** Walls and open doors are both problems. Healthy boundaries are firm where needed and open where safe.

**Consent is everywhere.** Asking and accepting the answer applies to hugs, favours and conversations as much as to sex.

**Your body knows first.** Discomfort often shows up physically before you can explain it. Learning to notice it helps you spot a crossed line.

**Damage has personal and social causes.** Family, trauma and attachment shape your boundaries, and so do power and prejudice, which teach some groups that their limits do not matter.

**Respect goes both ways.** Setting your own boundaries includes accepting other people's no and owning it when you overstep.

## If you read one chapter

"How Our Boundaries Get Fucked Up," because it explains where boundary problems come from, both in families and in society, in a way most books on the subject skip.
