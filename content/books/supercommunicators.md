## In short

*Supercommunicators: How to Unlock the Secret Language of Connection* is by Charles Duhigg, the Pulitzer Prize-winning journalist behind *The Power of Habit*. His claim is that each discussion is really one of three conversations: a practical one about what we are deciding, an emotional one about how we feel, or a social one about who we are. The people he calls supercommunicators notice which one is happening and join the other person in it.

## Summary

The book opens with Felix Sigala, a veteran FBI negotiator who trained other agents. In 2014 a Defense Department research group interviewed him, and instead of lecturing them on theory he asked about their lives and told stories of his own until they were opening up to him. Felix's secret was not charm. He paid close attention to what kind of conversation the other person wanted to have. Duhigg then describes his own failures. When a colleague came to him about trouble on her team, he kept offering practical fixes when what she was trying to tell him was that the people on the team needed to respect each other more. At home he had similar trouble with his wife. He set out to learn why some people seem to connect with anyone.

That leads to the book's framework and its first rule, the matching principle. Conversations go well when people are in the same kind of conversation at the same time. If you are talking about logistics while I am upset, we will miss each other. Duhigg ties this to research on how the brains of people who are really connecting start to fall into sync. One early story follows Jim Lawler, a CIA case officer trying to recruit a nervous foreign agent. His early attempts failed, and he got better when he dropped the sales pitch and responded to what the man was feeling.

The first type is the "What's this really about?" conversation. Duhigg argues that every conversation is partly a negotiation over what each person wants and how they want to decide. His main example is a jury in Wisconsin deliberating over Leroy Reed, a former convict who had bought a gun he was not allowed to own. The jurors split between applying the law strictly and considering Reed's limited understanding. A juror named John Boly, a literature professor, helped the group by asking questions that let each person say what mattered to them, and the jury reached agreement. A second story follows a surgeon, Behfar Ehdaie, who struggled to persuade prostate cancer patients to choose monitoring over risky surgery. Statistics did not work. Asking patients what they feared and hoped for did. Here Duhigg sets out his four rules for a "learning conversation": pay attention to what kind of conversation is happening, share your goals and ask about theirs, ask about feelings and share your own, and explore whether identities matter.

The second type is "How do we feel?" Duhigg reports on research by the psychologist Nicholas Epley showing that people are poor at guessing what others feel and do much better when they simply ask. He covers the "fast friends" study, where strangers who took turns answering thirty-six increasingly personal questions became surprisingly close. Deep questions about values, beliefs and experiences, he argues, invite people to open up, and when someone shows vulnerability, matching it builds trust. He adds a technique called looping for understanding: ask a question, repeat back what you heard in your own words, and check whether you got it right. He applies it to conflict, including conversations between people on opposite sides of gun control, where showing that you understand the other person often matters more than persuading them.

The third type is "Who are we?" Our social identities shape what we believe and whom we trust. Duhigg revisits the 1954 Robbers Cave experiment, in which boys at a summer camp in Oklahoma split into two groups and quickly became hostile. He describes a paediatrician, Jay Rosenbloom, who had more success with vaccine-hesitant parents when he spoke to them as fellow parents than when he cited data. Reminding people that they have many identities, and finding one you share, makes hard conversations easier. He also looks at Netflix's company-wide conversations about race and inclusion, and at how to handle identity in discussions online, where tone is easy to lose.

The book closes by returning to Duhigg's own home and work, where he tries the tools himself. His conclusion is that communication is a skill, and that the most important part of it is listening for what kind of conversation the other person is in.

## Key ideas

**Three kinds of conversation.** Practical ("What's this really about?"), emotional ("How do we feel?") and social ("Who are we?"). Most misunderstandings happen when people are in different ones.

**The matching principle.** Figure out which kind of conversation is happening and meet the other person there before trying to move it somewhere else.

**Every conversation is a negotiation.** People want different things and decide in different ways. Ask what each person wants and how they want to decide.

**Ask deep questions.** Questions about values, feelings and experiences get past small talk and invite real connection, even between strangers.

**Looping for understanding.** Ask, repeat back what you heard, and check you got it. It proves you are listening and calms conflict.

**Identities shape trust.** People listen more easily to someone who shares an identity with them. Highlighting shared or multiple identities can soften a standoff.

**Supercommunicators are made.** The skill comes from habits of attention and questioning, and anyone can learn them.

## If you read one chapter

The jury chapter about Leroy Reed, because it shows all the book's ideas at work in one real, tense room.
