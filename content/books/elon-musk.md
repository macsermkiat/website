## In short

Walter Isaacson spent two years at Musk's side, sitting in on meetings at SpaceX and Tesla and staying close through the Twitter takeover, and this book is the result. It tells the story in short chapters, from a hard childhood in South Africa to the rockets, the cars and the social network. The question under all of it is whether the drive that built these companies can be separated from the cruelty and chaos that come with it.

## Summary

The book opens in Pretoria, where Musk was born in 1971. He was a bookish, socially awkward boy who was bullied badly; once a group of boys beat him so hard he ended up in hospital. The worse damage, as Isaacson tells it, came from his father, Errol, who could be charming one moment and verbally savage the next. Elon chose to live with him after his parents divorced, and spent years regretting it. Isaacson keeps returning to this: the flat emotional affect, the sudden cold storms of anger that his partner Grimes called "demon mode", and the sense that Musk only feels alive in a crisis.

At seventeen he left for Canada, studied at Queen's University and then the University of Pennsylvania, and in 1995 started Zip2, an online maps-and-directory business, with his brother Kimbal. They slept in the office. Its sale made him a millionaire, and he put most of the money into X.com, an online bank that merged with a rival to become PayPal. In 2000 his colleagues removed him as CEO while he was away on his honeymoon. He kept his stake, and when eBay bought PayPal he had a fortune to gamble.

He gambled it on two long shots. After failing to buy old Russian missiles to send a greenhouse to Mars, he decided to build rockets himself and founded SpaceX in 2002. He also became the main investor in a small electric car company, Tesla, and later took it over. Isaacson spends a lot of time on 2008, the worst year. The first three launches of the Falcon 1 rocket failed. The fourth, in September, reached orbit. By December both companies were almost out of money and his first marriage was over. Then, within days of each other just before Christmas, NASA gave SpaceX a large cargo contract and a last-minute funding round saved Tesla.

From here the book becomes a record of how Musk works. He calls himself chief engineer at every company, walks factory floors, and asks why each part exists. Isaacson sets out his five-step "algorithm" for engineering and production, and shows it through episodes like the 2018 "production hell" of the Tesla Model 3, when Musk slept at the Fremont factory and had an assembly line built in a tent in the car park. He also shows the Starship "surge" of 2021, when Musk pulled hundreds of workers to Texas to stack the rocket in ten days because he was angry to find the launch pad empty on a Friday night.

The later chapters widen out. There are Starlink, Neuralink, the Boring Company, a humanoid robot, and his fear of artificial intelligence, which led him to help start OpenAI and later xAI. There are his relationships and his many children. There is a chapter on Starlink and the war in Ukraine; after publication Isaacson corrected his account of one episode there. Then comes the purchase of Twitter in 2022 for about $44 billion. Isaacson was in the room as Musk fired most of the staff and demanded that the rest be "hardcore", and he shows how impulsive moves, such as hastily moving servers out of a Sacramento data centre, caused months of trouble.

The book ends with the first full Starship test flight in April 2023, which blew up a few minutes after launch, and Musk treating the explosion as a success. Isaacson's own view stays close to the surface: Musk's recklessness and his achievements come from the same place, and you may not be able to have one without the other.

## Key ideas

**The algorithm.** Musk's five rules, in order: question every requirement, delete any part or process you can, simplify what is left, speed it up, and only then automate. He says he has lost years doing them in the wrong order, like automating steps that should have been deleted.

**Requirements have names.** Every rule should come with the name of the person who made it, not a department, so it can be questioned. Rules from smart people are the most dangerous, because nobody challenges them.

**Delete until it hurts.** If you don't end up adding back about ten percent of what you removed, you didn't remove enough.

**The idiot index.** Compare what a part costs with what its raw materials cost. A high ratio means the design or the process is wasteful, and that is where to dig.

**Maniacal urgency.** Musk sets deadlines he knows are impossible and treats every project as if the company is about to run out of money. It gets results and burns people out.

**Demon mode.** The musician Grimes's name, which Isaacson adopts, for the cold, furious state he enters under pressure. The book traces it to a childhood of bullying and a harsh father.

**Risk as fuel.** Musk seeks out bet-the-company moments and seems restless without them. The 2008 near-collapse of both SpaceX and Tesla is the book's central example.

**The package deal.** Isaacson's closing argument is that the traits that make Musk destructive are tied to the ones that make him effective, and that asking for one without the other may be asking for a different person.

## If you read one chapter

Read the chapters on 2008, when three rocket failures, a fourth-launch success and a Christmas rescue of both companies show Musk's appetite for risk at its most extreme.
