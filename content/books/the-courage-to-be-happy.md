## In short

Three years after the first book, the young man comes back to the philosopher's study. He has quit his job at a university library to teach at a school, tried to run his class on Adler's principles, and watched it fall apart, so he has come to announce that Adler is useless in the real world. Over one long night the philosopher answers him, and the book becomes a practical guide to Adler's psychology: respect, why praise and punishment backfire, and how independence leads to work, friendship and, finally, love.

## Summary

The youth arrives in a fury. Following the first book, he stopped praising and scolding his pupils, and the classroom descended into noise and disorder. He now thinks Adler's ideas are nice words that collapse on contact with children. If the philosopher cannot convince him tonight, he says, he will throw Adler out and never come back.

The philosopher begins with the goal of education, which in Adler's view is self-reliance: helping a child become able to live on their own terms while getting along with others. The starting point is respect. Following Erich Fromm, the philosopher defines respect as seeing a person as they are, without trying to change them. It means taking an interest in what interests the child, even if it seems trivial to an adult, and trying to see with their eyes.

Next comes a small drawing, a triangular prism. On one face is written "that bad person," on another "poor me." Most of what we say when we talk about our troubles falls on these two faces: complaints about someone who wronged us and pity for ourselves. The philosopher turns the prism to reveal the third face: "what should I do from now on?" That is the only question worth discussing, he says, and it is the one a counsellor or a teacher should steer towards.

The youth wants to know how to handle misbehaving pupils. The philosopher sets out five stages of problem behaviour, each a more desperate attempt to find a place in the group. First comes the demand for admiration, being a model pupil to win praise. If that fails, the child seeks attention, by clowning or breaking small rules. Then comes the power struggle, open defiance of adults. Then revenge, where the child tries to be hated because hatred is at least a kind of connection. The last stage is proof of incompetence: the child acts hopeless so that no one will expect anything from them. Scolding feeds the middle stages, because it gives the child the attention or the fight they want.

That leads to the claim the youth finds hardest to accept: praise does harm too. When a teacher rewards good work, pupils learn to work for the reward and to compete with one another for it. The classroom turns into a contest with a ranking. The philosopher wants a classroom run as a democracy, where rules are agreed together and the pupils see one another as comrades, not rivals. Instead of praise, a teacher should show appreciation and trust the child to take on their own tasks.

The talk then widens beyond school to Adler's three "life tasks": work, friendship and love. People work together because, as individuals, humans are physically weak; the division of labour is how we survived. Work relationships therefore rest on usefulness, and a person's value at work is not the same as their value as a human being. Friendship asks more: you have to offer confidence without conditions. The philosopher separates trust, which asks for collateral like a bank loan, from confidence, which believes in the other person with no guarantee. You should give first, he says, without waiting to see whether the other will give back.

The last and hardest task is love. The philosopher argues that we choose our way of life very early, around the question of how to be loved by our parents. Growing up means turning that around: learning to love rather than to be loved. Love is not falling for a destined partner. There is no one person meant for you. It is a decision to build a life together, in which the subject stops being "I" and becomes "we."

By dawn the youth has calmed down. He decides to go back to his classroom and try again. The philosopher tells him they should not meet like this any more, because they are now equals and the youth must walk his own road.

## Key ideas

**Education aims at self-reliance.** A teacher's job is to make themselves unnecessary, helping children decide things for themselves and live well among others.

**Respect means seeing people as they are.** Drawing on Erich Fromm, the book defines respect as accepting someone without conditions and taking an interest in what they care about.

**The triangular prism.** Complaints about "that bad person" and "poor me" lead nowhere. The useful question is "what should I do from now on?"

**Five stages of problem behaviour.** Misbehaving children move from seeking praise, to seeking attention, to power struggles, to revenge, to acting helpless. Each is a bid to belong, and scolding feeds it.

**No praise, no punishment.** Both put the adult above the child and turn a group into a competition. Appreciation and agreed rules work better.

**Trust versus confidence.** Trust wants guarantees. Confidence believes in someone without them, and friendship depends on it.

**Love is a decision, not destiny.** There is no soulmate waiting to be found. Love is something two people choose to build, moving from "I" to "we."

## If you read one chapter

The part on reward and punishment, because it is where the book's argument about praise and competition is laid out most clearly, and it is the claim that most challenges ordinary habits at home and at school.
