## In short

A young man visits a philosopher in a thousand-year-old city to prove him wrong about his claim that the world is simple and anyone can be happy. Over five nights the philosopher explains the ideas of Alfred Adler, the Austrian psychologist who broke with Freud: the past does not decide your life, all problems are about other people, and freedom means being willing to be disliked. Ichiro Kishimi is a Japanese Adler scholar and Fumitake Koga is the writer who shaped their talks into a Socratic dialogue.

## Summary

The youth arrives angry. He grew up in the shadow of a brilliant older brother, dislikes himself, and thinks the philosopher's optimism is naive. The book follows their arguments night by night, with the youth often storming off and coming back.

On the first night the philosopher sets out Adler's most provocative idea. Freud looked for causes in the past, such as trauma. Adler looked at goals. The youth offers the example of a friend who has shut himself in his room for years and says anxiety keeps him there. The philosopher turns it around: the friend creates the anxiety because he has decided, for now, not to go out, and staying in keeps him safe from being judged. In the same way, someone who shouts at a waiter who spilled coffee on them has not been overcome by anger. They produced the anger because they wanted to shout. Trauma, in this view, does not exist as a cause. What matters is the meaning we give to our experiences, and that we can change.

The second night argues that all problems are interpersonal. Feelings of inferiority are normal and can push us to grow, but they turn into an inferiority complex when we use them as an excuse. The philosopher describes a young woman who asked him to cure her blushing so she could tell a man she liked him. He saw that the blushing was useful to her: as long as she had it, she never had to risk rejection. Adler called such excuses "life-lies." The philosopher also warns against treating life as a competition, where every other person is a rival and every argument is a power struggle.

On the third night comes the book's central tool, the separation of tasks. For any problem, ask whose task it is, meaning who will bear the consequences. What your parents think of your career choice is their task. Whether a child studies is ultimately the child's task. You can offer help, but you should not force it, and you should not let others meddle in yours. The desire for recognition is the trap here: if you live to satisfy other people's expectations, you are living their life. Real freedom, the philosopher says, means accepting that some people will dislike you. The philosopher tells how he decided to rebuild his relationship with his own difficult father, without waiting for his father to change.

The fourth night introduces community feeling, the sense of belonging to others and caring about them. The philosopher says that people who worry constantly about how they look to others are, oddly, self-centred. He rejects both praise and rebuke, because both come from above and treat the other person as inferior. Instead he wants horizontal relationships, in which people are equals, and encouragement, such as a simple "thank you." A person feels worthwhile when they feel useful to others.

The last night brings the pieces together. Self-acceptance means accepting what you cannot change and working on what you can. Confidence in others means trusting people unconditionally, even at the risk of being betrayed. Contribution to others means feeling you are of use, which Adler saw as the root of happiness. The youth asks whether he must do something special. The philosopher replies that he needs the courage to be normal. Life, he says, is not a line leading to a goal but a series of moments, like a dance, and each moment is complete in itself. The youth leaves, won over at last.

## Key ideas

**Teleology, not trauma.** Adler holds that we act for present goals rather than being pushed by past causes. The past matters only through the meaning you choose to give it.

**Lifestyle is chosen.** Your way of seeing the world and yourself is a choice, often made in childhood. You can make it again, though changing takes courage.

**All problems are interpersonal.** Loneliness, envy, inferiority and anger all involve other people. Even self-dislike, the philosopher says, is a way of avoiding relationships.

**Separation of tasks.** Ask who ultimately bears the result of a choice. Do your own tasks and let others do theirs, without intruding or letting them intrude.

**The courage to be disliked.** Needing everyone's approval makes you unfree. Being willing to be disliked is the price of living your own life.

**Horizontal relationships.** Treat others as equals. Encourage and thank them instead of praising or scolding from above.

**Happiness is contribution.** Feeling useful to others, even without their recognition, gives a person a sense of worth.

**Live here and now.** Life is a series of moments. Treat each as complete rather than as a step towards some distant goal.

## If you read one chapter

The third night, "Discard Other People's Tasks," because the separation of tasks is the idea from the book that is easiest to use the next day.
