## In short

Modern finance is built on the assumption that price changes follow the bell curve, like a series of coin tosses. Benoit Mandelbrot, writing with the financial journalist Richard Hudson, shows with decades of data that this is false: big jumps are far more common, and calm and stormy periods cluster together. He offers fractal geometry as a better description of how markets move and warns that the standard tools badly underestimate risk.

## Summary

The book opens with a few blunt rules drawn from Mandelbrot's lifetime of study. Markets are riskier than we think, trouble comes in streaks, markets have a kind of personality, they mislead, and market time does not run at the speed of clock time.

Part One explains the orthodox view and takes it apart. Mandelbrot contrasts two kinds of randomness. Mild randomness is like tossing a coin: no single toss changes the average much, and extreme results are vanishingly rare. Wild randomness is like a blindfolded archer shooting at a wall that stretches forever. Most arrows land nearby, but now and then one lands so far away that it dominates the average on its own. Standard finance assumes the first kind. Mandelbrot believes markets are the second.

He traces the mild view back to Louis Bachelier, a French student who in 1900 wrote a thesis modelling prices on the Paris exchange as a random walk. The idea was forgotten for half a century, then rediscovered by economists like Paul Samuelson and built into what Mandelbrot calls the house of modern finance: Harry Markowitz's portfolio theory, William Sharpe's capital asset pricing model, and the Black-Scholes formula for pricing options. All of them rest on the bell curve, and on assumptions that investors are rational and alike and that prices move smoothly.

The data say otherwise. By the bell curve, from 1916 to 2003 the Dow Jones index should have moved more than 3.4 percent in a day on about 58 days. It did so on 1,001. The model treats a 7 percent day as a once-in-300,000-years event; the Dow had 48 of them in the twentieth century. Such numbers turn "once in a lifetime" disasters into regular events.

Part Two builds the alternative. Mandelbrot explains fractals, shapes that look similar at every scale, like a coastline or a fern, which he had been studying since the 1960s. His turn to finance began with cotton. In the early 1960s he analysed decades of cotton price records and found that the pattern of daily, monthly and yearly changes looked alike when rescaled, and that big changes followed a power law, not a bell curve. Economists were then busy adopting the orthodox models, and his findings were largely set aside.

Next comes long memory. The British hydrologist Harold Edwin Hurst, who spent his career on the Nile, found that the river's floods and droughts ran in long streaks, with wet years tending to follow wet years. Mandelbrot saw the same persistence in prices. He named the two kinds of wildness after Bible stories. The Noah effect is the sudden catastrophic jump, like the flood. The Joseph effect is the long run of good or bad years, like Joseph's seven fat and seven lean years in Egypt. Together they produce the booms and busts markets actually show.

He then introduces "trading time." In a crisis, markets seem to speed up, packing a year of movement into a few days; in quiet periods the clock slows. His multifractal model stretches and compresses time this way, and its simulated price charts are hard to tell apart from real ones.

Part Three draws out the lessons in ten "heresies." Markets are turbulent and very risky. Much of the gain or loss comes in a few short bursts, so timing matters more than buy-and-hold advice admits. Prices jump rather than glide. The same patterns appear in all markets and all periods. Bubbles and crashes are built in. Charts deceive the eye, so patterns traders see are often illusions. Prices cannot be forecast, but volatility can be, because big moves tend to follow big moves. And the idea of "value" is less useful than people assume. A final chapter visits researchers and traders, such as Richard Olsen and Jean-Philippe Bouchaud, who were already applying these methods, and calls for new tools for portfolios, option pricing and risk management.

## Key ideas

**Mild versus wild randomness.** In mild randomness, like coin tosses, extremes are negligible. In wild randomness, a single extreme event can outweigh everything else, and Mandelbrot argues that markets behave this way.

**Fat tails.** Big price moves happen far more often than the bell curve predicts. Models that ignore this treat routine crises as nearly impossible.

**Scaling.** Price charts look similar whether you view a day, a month or a year. The same mathematical rules describe changes at every time scale.

**The Noah effect.** Prices often leap discontinuously instead of moving smoothly. There may be no chance to sell on the way down.

**The Joseph effect.** Markets have long memory: calm periods and turbulent ones cluster, so today's volatility says something about tomorrow's.

**Trading time.** Market time speeds up in crises and slows in quiet spells. Measuring risk in clock time misses this.

**Volatility is forecastable, prices are not.** You cannot predict which way a price will move, but you can estimate how rough the ride is likely to be.

## If you read one chapter

The chapter "Ten Heresies of Finance," because it gathers the whole argument into ten plain claims about how markets really behave.
